# Chapter 11

The colony was already doing what colonies do after something happens to them: moving on.

That was the thing about DE — you could see the recalibration in real time. Equipment back in operation. Shift schedules posted, as if they hadn't been posted twelve hours ago and half the people reading them had spent the night running from something. The faces were tighter than usual. The conversations had that particular quality of people who have agreed, without discussing it, that certain things are better not examined in the open. It was survival behavior. Jax recognized it because he had been doing it for eleven years.

He was packed before the second shift bell.

There was less than he expected to have. A miner's kit, maintained and functional. The tin box, because he had never once left it behind in eleven years and wasn't about to start now. A change of clothes, a canteen, tools that fit in a bag. The shard was on his person — inside pocket, where he could feel it when he breathed.

He checked it before he left the room. Not from habit — he checked the tin box from habit. This was something else: a need to verify the thing was still the thing he knew. The flare in the fight last night had left it different. Cooler than usual, the warmth that was always faintly there gone quiet, the metal reading closer to the temperature of his skin than its own. He held it a moment. It warmed slowly, pulling something from him to do it, the way a cold tool pulls warmth from the hand holding it. That was new. He filed it and put it back in the inside pocket.

He did not look back at his bunk. There was nothing there worth the look.

---

Ferris was outside the equipment depot when Jax passed it, the way Ferris was always outside the equipment depot at this hour, doing the kind of slow end-of-shift inventory that takes twice as long as it needs to because the person doing it has learned not to hurry. Thirty years on DE. No past worth mentioning, no future he'd ever named. He looked at Jax the way he looked at everything — with the patient attention of a man who has watched a great many things come through a place and knows the difference between the ones that are passing through and the ones that have already left.

He didn't ask where Jax was going. He said, "Bag's packed light."

"It is," Jax said.

Ferris nodded, the way a man nods when a thing he expected to happen has happened. He turned back to the inventory. That was all.

Jax kept walking. He didn't know why that exchange sat differently than he'd expected it to. He moved on.

At the edge of the Keeper Quarter he stopped without planning to.

He had passed through this part of the colony hundreds of times. The small cluster of families who had been here longer than most, who kept to themselves without being unfriendly, whose children played in a slightly different register than the other children on DE — quieter, or maybe just more watchful. He had never thought much about them.

One of the women was standing at her doorway. Late sixties, probably. He'd seen her before without registering her. She looked at him now with the particular quality of someone watching a thing arrive that they have been expecting for a long time. Not surprise. Something closer to relief, and underneath it something he couldn't name.

She said nothing. She didn't need to.

He kept moving. The thought that came with him — *she knew* — he put down with the same deliberate motion he'd use to set down a tool he didn't have time for yet.

---

The landing pad smelled like fuel and the particular mineral dust that everything on DE eventually smelled like, as if the planet was coating the things that tried to leave. Toby Blackwood's ship was already being readied — not hurried, just methodical. Toby was the kind of pilot who maintained his equipment because it was what stood between him and the alternative, and the attention showed. The ship was old, not new, but it had the functional density of something that had been kept.

Toby saw Jax coming and said, "Good morning," in the voice of a man who was prepared for that to be an optimistic assessment.

Jax said, "Is it."

"Marginally. You ready?"

"Does it look like I'm not ready?"

Toby looked at the bag on Jax's shoulder and the particular set of his posture and said, "You've been ready since you woke up."

This was accurate and therefore not worth arguing about. Jax moved to stow his bag. Toby, without being asked, made room in the cargo bay that was larger than what was needed for one bag, and did not comment on this. He had his own reasons for leaving DE, reasons he had not stated last night and did not state now, and his willingness to provide transit without demanding the kind of disclosure he was not offering was the most practical kind of trust — not personal, not yet, but structural. It would do.

Mira Stavros arrived at the pad while they were still in the preliminary logistics.

She had her kit in a single bag, compact and well-organized in the way of someone who had learned to carry everything important and leave everything that wasn't. Her last security contract had ended with the attack — technically ended, Jax supposed, when the outfit she'd been contracted to stopped having a physical plant to protect. She had the steady-eyed look of someone who had already processed this and moved on to the next practical question.

She did not ask for permission to board. She said, "I need transit out of DE. I'll work for passage." Brief. No elaboration. No terms beyond that.

Jax looked at her for a moment — the way he read terrain, or the structural integrity of an unfamiliar shaft. He had watched her last night without planning to. The things she had done in the attack that nobody had asked her to do. The angles she had covered. The way she thought in terms of who needed not to get hurt rather than waiting for a threat to become direct.

He nodded. She boarded.

Toby, watching this, said nothing. He had the wisdom of someone who knew when not to interrupt an agreement that was working.

---

In transit, the ship felt small in the useful way — small enough that the dynamic established itself before any of them had decided what they wanted it to be. Toby talked. Not about anything consequential: the route, the fuel efficiency of the current vector, the particular quirk of the stabilizers he had fixed twice and expected to fix again. He talked the way some people fill a room, easily and without demanding reciprocation, so that silence never had time to become awkward.

At one point he said, conversationally, "Funny thing about navigation. You pick a heading and you commit, and then later you find out why it was the right heading. Never before." He was looking at the instrument panel when he said it. He didn't look at Jax.

Mira said almost nothing. She had found a position where she could see both the entrance to the rear cabin and the instrument panel simultaneously, which was not the most comfortable seat available, and had settled into it with the quality of someone prepared to stay there indefinitely. When the cargo bay made a sound an hour into transit — a minor shift, a crate resettling — she was on her feet and checking it before Jax had registered the noise. She came back, sat down, said nothing about it.

Probably she wasn't even aware she'd done it.

Jax sat somewhere between them. He said less than Toby and more than Mira and this turned out to be the natural register of the thing they were becoming without yet being ready to name it.

He checked the tin box once, the way he always checked it. The shard. The scrap of fabric. The locket.

He opened it further than usual. He didn't know why. Inside the lid, barely legible where the hinge met the metal, was a small mark he had never been able to make out clearly — not quite a letter, not quite a number, worn to the threshold of meaning by eleven years of handling. He had looked at it before. Today he looked at it longer, as if it might resolve into something if he gave it enough time.

It didn't. He closed the box.

The shard in his inside pocket was oriented. Not pulling — Stellarium didn't pull him the way it apparently pulled Drayton, fever-hot and consuming. It was more like a compass finding north: quiet acknowledgment that the direction they were moving was the right direction. Warmer now than it had been this morning, whatever it had drawn from him restored in some incremental way. He had been moving away from DE for six hours and the signal had not once produced doubt.

He did not examine what that meant.

---

The first transit stop beyond DE was the kind of place that existed because routes had to intersect somewhere and some intersections accumulate, over time, enough infrastructure to be briefly useful to people who need to be briefly unremarkable. It was not a destination. It was a between-place, and it knew this about itself.

Jax was at the transit desk handling the logistics of the next leg — fuel, route confirmation, an argument with a dispatcher about transit fees that was less about the fees and more about establishing that he was a routine traveler with routine business — when he heard footsteps behind him with the quality of a comfortable, unhurried walk toward a meeting that had always been scheduled.

He turned.

The man crossing the landing area toward him was somewhere in his thirties, with the easy physicality of someone who worked with his hands and the particular studied unimpressedness of someone who had been told something significant and reserved judgment on whether it would live up to the description. He had a mechanic's bag over one shoulder. He was arriving, not approaching.

"Jax Tarkin." Not a question.

Jax looked at him. "Who's asking."

"Quill Anders." He stopped a comfortable distance away, unhurried. "I'm between jobs. I have a particular set of skills with older-model Varr-adjacent technology that tends to come up in certain kinds of situations." A pause. "Not going to say more than that here."

"Who sent you."

"Nobody sent me. I was told to be here." The distinction, in his voice, was deliberate. "There's a difference."

Jax studied him the way he'd studied Mira at the landing pad — reading for the structural truth. What he found was someone who was not performing comfort. Someone who had decided what they were willing to do, made peace with it, and arrived at the other side of that decision intact. He wasn't selling anything. He wasn't asking for anything. He had simply calculated himself into the situation as if the math had already been done.

Behind Jax, Toby's voice came from somewhere near the ship: "Huh." And then, with barely contained amusement: "Does this happen to you a lot."

Mira said nothing. She was watching Quill the way she watched all new additions to any situation — with the comprehensive attention of someone cataloguing variables.

Jax turned back to the dispatcher and completed the fuel arrangement. He paid the transit fee without the argument, because he had stopped caring about the transit fee.

He did not turn back to Quill for another moment. When he did, Quill was standing near the edge of the landing area, not where Jax had left him. He had moved — a few meters, casually — and was now positioned at an angle to the dock where he could see both the ship's access ramp and the transit desk without appearing to watch either. He wasn't looking at anything in particular. He was looking at everything.

Jax clocked this without comment. He said, "Varr-adjacent technology."

"Among other things."

"Can you shoot."

"When necessary."

"Can you stay quiet."

Quill looked at him steadily. "I managed not to talk the last three minutes."

This was, Jax allowed internally, a reasonable point.

He picked up his bag. He jerked his chin toward the ship. Quill fell into step beside him without ceremony, without visible triumph, without any indication that this had resolved in a direction he'd been tracking.

Halfway to the ramp Quill said, without breaking stride: "The dock worker by the fuel intake. Third from the left. He's been watching your ship since we started talking." A beat. "Probably nothing. Worth knowing."

Jax glanced. The man was doing his job. He was also, if you were paying attention, not quite doing only his job. Jax filed it.

*Useful.*

---

The team had its shape. He hadn't recruited any of them. He had agreed to each of them — which was, he told himself, a different thing. His call. His judgment, applied to each one. Toby because Toby had a ship and had covered people in the fight without being asked. Mira because she was already functioning as protection before she had a reason to. Quill because Varr-adjacent technical expertise was going to be necessary and this particular person had crossed a landing pad with the confidence of someone who had already decided to be useful — and had spent their first three minutes in a new location identifying who was watching.

His choices. He had made them.

He walked up the ramp.

He was most of the way to the cockpit when the thought arrived, quiet and uninvited: *Nalani had told him to find her. The Keeper Quarter on DE had been expecting something — that woman at the doorway had not been surprised to see him go, had been waiting for it. And Quill was at this stop — the one Jax had chosen for practical reasons, fuel and routing — waiting.*

He did not stop walking.

He turned it over once more, which he didn't usually allow himself. The shard had come to him before any of this — before Nalani, before the call. Eleven years of checking the tin box. Eleven years of a conviction he could not explain and called determination. Was that a choice? He had never decided to carry the shard. He had just never once put it down.

He pushed the question down to somewhere it wouldn't interfere with the next practical problem.

Behind him, Quill was already asking Toby about the engine configuration with the focused interest of someone who had found a new problem to solve. Toby's answer stretched into the particular register he used when he was genuinely interested in his own subject.

Somewhere in his inside pocket the shard was warm. Moving in the right direction.

*I've dug through worse.*

He kept moving.
