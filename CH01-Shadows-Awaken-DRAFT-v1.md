# Chapter 1 — Really Bad Day

## Scene 1 — The Dream

The flash comes first. Always the flash.

Then the pain: not pain exactly, but the sensation of being erased, every signal cutting at once, every nerve going silent before it can report. Then the nothing.

Not darkness. Jax had learned the difference in the first year, lying on the floor of this same room while the walls did something that felt like spinning. Darkness was something the body knew how to hold. It was an absence of light, which meant it still had edges, still had the particular weight of a thing that existed. The nothing was an absence of everything, sensation and self and the low hum of being a person, and it lasted long enough for some part of him to notice that this was not sleep.

He woke.

The ceiling of his quarters was pale concrete, cracked at the same diagonal line it had always been cracked at, carrying the same faint water stain from a leak fixed eight years ago. The air smelled the way the air always smelled: metal and mineral dust and the particular flatness of recycled atmosphere that never quite cleared. He lay still for a moment, not to recover but because he'd learned the floor felt wrong if he moved too fast after the dream.

Three breaths. He counted them. Then he sat up.

The tin box was on the shelf where it always was.

Four steps to reach it; he could have made them blind. He lifted the lid. Inside: the locket first, because it always appeared to him first. Oval, silver gone warm with handling, the surface worn smooth at the center where his thumb had rested ten thousand times without opening it. Beside it, the shard of Stellarium. Dull gray in the pre-shift dark, inert as river stone when it sat in the box, which it always did when it sat in the box, and he had stopped wondering about that distinction around year three. And behind the shard, the note, folded once, in handwriting that matched his own down to the way he closed his *e*s and left too much space before a period.

*You were looking for something. Keep looking.*

He'd had the handwriting verified by a specialist in his third year on Desolation's Edge. An expensive verification, for a note that told him nothing he didn't already know about himself and nothing at all about what he'd been looking for. He didn't have the credits back then for things that didn't pan out. He didn't have them now, technically, but he'd stopped caring about that distinction somewhere around year seven.

He closed the box. He had work to do.

---

The pre-shift meal was a habit, not a pleasure. Whatever was cheapest at the commissary canteen, eaten standing up at the counter near the door so he could leave without navigating the room. Desolation's Edge woke in shifts; at this hour the canteen held a specific subset of miners, the ones who liked the early dark before the heat. He knew their faces. He didn't know most of their names. The ones who'd been here long enough to accept that about him had stopped trying to change it, and that was the closest thing to friendship the Edge reliably produced.

He ate standing up. He left.

Outside, the morning held the cold Xanthius kept until midday, dry cold with teeth but no real malice, the kind that cracked his knuckles in the first hour of winter and that he'd grown to use as a calibration. If his hands hurt, they were working right. He pulled his collar up and moved toward the mine entrance at the pace he'd maintained for eleven years: not hurrying, not slow, the efficiency of someone who'd learned not to spend what he didn't have to.

Desolation's Edge from the outside had no pretensions. The mine head broke the skyline a kilometer beyond the town's main cluster: the crane rigging, the ore-processing towers, the two conveyors running parallel toward the crushing facility, all of it built for function in a climate that wore everything down. The town existed because the mine existed, the mine existed because Stellarium deposits in the region had drawn enough rumor to keep prospectors coming, and enough real ore to keep the corporate contracts renewing. Most people who came to DE looking for Stellarium left with nothing but the years. Some of them eventually stopped trying to leave.

Jax had stopped trying to leave. He hadn't stopped looking for Stellarium.

## Scene 2 — The Mines

The mine head at shift change was controlled noise: the outgoing crew moving past the incoming crew that hadn't yet woken enough to fill the space. Jax checked his equipment at the locker station. Headlamp charge, oxygen reserve, the geological scanner he'd been repairing in pieces for eighteen months because it was the best scanner the Edge had available and the company wouldn't supply a replacement. The shard he transferred from the tin box to the inside pocket of his jacket, where it traveled close to his ribs during working hours. He'd stopped explaining that habit too.

The section assigned to him today was a lateral survey of a new formation the automated systems had flagged as potentially unstable. Routine work. Jax preferred lateral bores to the deep runs; the deep runs were where the interesting Stellarium traces had been showing lately, but the lateral work gave him what he needed: something the body could do while the rest of him ran the longer calculations in the background. Reading instability in geological formations was something he did the way other people read a room. Continuous, mostly unconscious, registering wrongness before he could name it. Whatever else he'd forgotten about who he'd been, he hadn't forgotten this.

He dropped into the survey section at 0610.

Three hours of measurements. He flagged stress concentrations in the rock face for the engineering team, made the calls that were his to make, annotated the ones that needed escalation. The air in a lateral bore was different from the air at depth: not thinner, but closer, the sense of rock pressing in from all sides rather than just above. Some miners found it worse. For Jax it was useful. The closer the rock, the more it said.

He was three hours into the second measurement pass when the commotion started forty meters back, toward the junction post.

He heard it before he could parse it: a sharp metallic impact, the specific register of something falling that wasn't supposed to fall, and then a voice that broke wrong underneath it. He set down his equipment and moved.

The miner was young; Jax had seen his face twice in the commissary and placed him at six months on the Edge, maybe seven. He'd caught his drill brace in a shear clamp that had shifted. Not catastrophic, the kind of thing that happened in lateral bores when someone who hadn't learned yet tried to go faster than the formation allowed. The drill was running, the brace had locked, and the miner was torquing against it with the particular desperation of someone who understood the problem but had understood it too late.

If the brace snapped, the drill would kick. At that angle, into that formation, it would kick left.

Jax came in from the opposite side of the brace, used his weight to pin the drill steady, and found the override lever with his right hand without looking for it. A mechanism most miners knew existed and half had never located because knowing where something was and knowing where it was in the dark were different things. He found it. He depressed it. The drill disengaged.

The miner straightened, breathing fast. He started to say something.

"Pay attention to the brace angle when the formation's been flagged," Jax said, already turning back toward his equipment. "Shear zones announce themselves. Listen for them."

He picked up the scanner. He went back to work.

He lost the time it took, maybe eight minutes, maybe twelve, and he'd need to run the last segment of the measurement pass again. He noted that in his log without annotation and continued.

The rest of the shift ran without incident. Jax made no small talk on the way out. He turned in his equipment readings, flagged two of his annotations as priority, and walked back through the mine head into the afternoon.

## Scene 3 — End of Day / The Shard

The sun over Desolation's Edge in the late afternoon came in low and amber, the way it always did when Heliosanthus was dropping toward the horizon and the mineral dust in the air caught it. It turned everything the color of old brass: the processing towers, the colony streets, the faces of the people walking back from shift. The light was specific enough that you couldn't mistake it for any other world, which was something Jax had noticed in his first week here and had kept noticing ever since in the way that things noticed early became part of how you measured time.

He walked back through the colony streets at the same pace he'd walked out in the morning. The streets ran parallel to the mine conveyors in the main working quarter, then widened and bent toward the residential blocks on the eastern side. He kept to the working quarter longer than he needed to. It was not a decision exactly, more a preference that had calcified into habit.

The Keeper Quarter was at the edge of town, a short cluster of low buildings on the northeast side where the street grid lost its logic and the structures became something other than mining infrastructure. They were older than most of the colony, or they looked it, and the people who came and went from them moved differently from the people in the working quarter, unhurried in a way that was not the same as idle. Jax had passed the Keeper Quarter on his way to and from the mine for eleven years. He had never stopped. He was not sure when he had started to notice that the people there seemed to notice him back.

He passed it without stopping, as he always did, and continued to his quarters.

---

Inside: the same pale concrete, the same shelf, the same box. He filled a glass of water from the tap and drank it standing at the window, watching the amber light go flat as the sun finished dropping. Then he pulled the tool kit from under the work bench and started on his scanner.

Cleaning equipment after a shift was meditative in the specific way that tasks requiring precision without thought were meditative: the hands busy, the attention low, the mind settling into whatever the day had left in it. He'd done this for eleven years, the tools changing as they wore out or broke and were replaced, but the motion the same. Some evenings nothing came up while he worked. Some evenings things did.

Tonight he wasn't thinking about anything in particular when the shard hummed.

He stopped.

It was barely a sound. Less a sound than a sensation, the way a vibration in the walls of a mine registered in the chest before the ears caught up with it. The shard was in his jacket, draped over the work bench's edge, and the jacket had not moved. He looked at it.

Nothing.

He waited. The room was quiet except for the ambient noise of the colony settling into its evening shift: somewhere distant a compressor cycling, the low frequency of the recycling units in the walls. Nothing that should have set anything off. Nothing that explained what he'd felt.

He crossed to the jacket and took the shard out. Held it in his palm.

Inert. Cool and smooth, the way it always was when it sat in the tin box or traveled in his pocket. He turned it over once. Pressed his thumb along its longest edge, the way he'd done ten thousand times. Nothing.

He stood there for a moment longer.

Then he set the shard on the shelf beside the tin box, went back to his bench, and finished cleaning his tools.

He did not open the tin box again that night. He had work to do in the morning, and the dream would come back whether he spent the evening thinking about it or whether he slept.

He slept.
