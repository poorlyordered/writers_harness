# Chapter 2 -- Mystery and Theme

## Scene 1 -- The Shard Responds

The morning began the way all mornings began at Desolation's Edge: the alarm at 0500, the overhead light coming on at a level that didn't ask permission, the specific weight of recycled air that told him before he was fully awake that nothing had changed while he slept.

He sat up. Swung his feet to the floor. Stood.

The tin box was on the shelf.

He had a system for mornings, and the system worked by not thinking. Wash up, dress, eat something at the canteen, check his equipment at the locker station, drop into the mine. The system held eleven years of mornings together the way the support struts held the mine shafts: not complicated, reliable, not worth examining too closely. He moved through it the way he moved through everything: efficiently, without waste.

He was three steps from the tin box when it hummed.

Not like last night. Last night had been brief, faint, the kind of thing his sleep-deprived brain might have produced without assistance. This was cleaner. Sustained. The hum had a quality to it that the one last night hadn't: direction. Like a compass needle that had been drifting for years finally settling toward north.

He stopped.

Stood still for a moment. The ambient sounds of the colony filled in around the silence: the recycling units cycling, a distant conveyor running its first pass of the morning, the low mechanical register of the mine head two kilometers north. Nothing unusual. Nothing that would produce the kind of resonance he'd just heard.

He crossed to the box and opened it.

The shard sat where it always sat, behind the locket and the folded note. He didn't look at the note. He hadn't opened the locket in three months -- he'd counted. He picked it up. Held it flat on his palm the way he always did, waiting for the response he usually got: nothing. The shard sitting inert and cool, gray as river stone in the flat overhead light.

The hum was real.

Low frequency, barely a vibration. He pressed his thumb along the longest edge and felt it more than heard it, a faint oscillation that had no business coming from a piece of mineral he'd been carrying for eleven years without incident. He tilted his hand. The sensation didn't follow the tilt the way something caused by the shard's own mass would. It was pointed at something. Something outside the room, outside the colony, somewhere in a direction he didn't have a name for.

He stood there for a full minute.

Then he ran through the possibilities the way he ran through every problem: methodically, building toward an explanation that satisfied. Vibration from the morning conveyor startup -- he'd felt that frequency in the walls before, a low rolling pressure that transmitted through the floor. This was not in the floor. This was in his hand. He stepped off the bedside mat and put his feet flat on the bare concrete, testing. The floor was quiet.

A new vein being worked in the adjacent shaft, the resonance traveling through the rock -- possible, worth checking, except the sensation was directional in a way that geological resonance wasn't. Geological resonance came from everywhere at once, diffuse, sourceless. This had a vector. He turned his hand slowly, keeping the shard level, and the sensation stayed consistent as he faced one direction and faded as he turned away from it. Not diffuse. Not geological.

Equipment interference from his scanner, still on the work bench -- he checked. The scanner was off. He checked the other tools. Nothing running, nothing drawing power.

No explanation found. He filed the absence of one, the way he'd learned to file the growing collection of things about the shard that had no explanation: noted, stored, not pursued until pursuing would tell him something.

He put the shard back. Closed the tin. Standing in his quarters analyzing a piece of mineral that had been inexplicable for eleven years was not going to make it explicable in the next ten minutes. He'd spent his first three years on DE trying. He'd learned to file it and move.

He went to work.
