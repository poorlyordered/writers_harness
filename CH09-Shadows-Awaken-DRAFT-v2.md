# Chapter 9 -- Refusal of the Call

## Scene 1 -- Return to DE

The return crossing was shorter than it should have been.

He noticed this when the transition zone resolved into navigable terrain less than two hours out from the valley's edge -- the same terrain that had taken him most of a day outbound, the same geological logic that had refused to cooperate with his instruments and his training and the directional certainty he'd spent eleven years building. Now it was just terrain. The visual phenomena were absent: no brightness at the periphery, no shadows that absorbed light instead of casting it, no landmarks that shifted position when he moved toward them. The weight in the air that had made certain directions harder than others was not present. The shard in his pocket was quiet -- warm in the baseline way it was always warm, nothing directional, nothing insistent.

He noticed all of this. He did not draw conclusions from it. He adjusted his heading for the return leg and kept moving.

DE resolved out of the plateau heat in the early afternoon, the way it always resolved: mine heads first, angular against the sky, then the smelter stacks, then the sprawl of residential structures that had accreted over decades without plan. The specific smell arrived before the visual -- machine oil, mineral dust, the particular undertone of a settlement that recycled its water more than it refreshed it, and underneath that the faint sulfur note from the eastern smelter that was always stronger when the wind came off the plateau. He had lived with this smell for eleven years. He had always found it reassuring in the way familiar things were reassuring, not because they were good but because they were known.

He found it less reassuring than he had before. Not unpleasant -- just insufficient in a way it hadn't been when he'd left. He noted this, classified it as a re-acclimation effect, and kept walking through the gate.

His quarters: the bunk, the shelf, the window that let in DE's amber light at the right hours. The journal on the table where he'd left it. The survey tools on their hooks, each in the position he always left them. The specific smell of his own space, inhabited and unchanged.

The tin box on the shelf.

He crossed to the shelf, took the shard from his jacket pocket, and put it in the tin box and closed the lid. The motion was deliberate and without ceremony -- the same motion he used to stow equipment at end-of-shift, functional and complete, the act of a person putting something in its place. He set the box back on the shelf. He did not open it again.

He unpacked the rest of the pack. He ate from his stores, something efficient and without much thought. He sat on the bunk for a while looking at nothing in particular, the specific stillness of a person who is not resting because they are tired. Then he lay down, because the alternative was to continue sitting, and stared at the ceiling until the space between one thought and the next grew large enough that sleep became unavoidable.

The dream came sometime in the early morning hours. The flash, the impact, the white. The face at the end of the white -- and this time the face had a specificity it had not had before: not new details, but a sharpness, the kind of definition that usually lasted only the first second of waking. He lay with it for three seconds before the specificity began to fade, and for three more while the fading completed, and then got up and went to work because the shift was starting and he had work to do and this was what he had come back to DE for.

The shard was quiet in its box. He left it there and went underground.

## Scene 2 -- Back to the Mines

The seam was where he had left it.

The geology of this section was familiar in the way only eleven years of daily contact produced -- not memorized but inhabited, the way a body inhabits a space until it makes the calculations automatically and stops being aware of making them. He knew how this rock behaved under load. He knew where the fracture planes ran and which faces were stable and which would need shoring before the next crew worked them. He could read this formation the way he read text, faster than instruments, with a fluency built from ten thousand shifts of paying the kind of attention that eventually becomes instinct.

He had this fluency before the shard arrived. The shard had made him better at it, amplified something already there into something that occasionally startled the other surveyors when they checked his work. He was currently not thinking about this.

He worked. The shift had its rhythm: extraction, survey, transit, documentation, repeat. The other miners on his section occupied their positions in the familiar way of people who share a working space without sharing much else -- a great deal of context and very little information. Reiss on the secondary face, who had been working this mine longer than Jax and moved with the unhurried precision of someone who had stopped proving anything to anyone. Dak running transit, efficient and minimal, the kind of worker who was never late and never early and never said more than was operationally necessary. The section supervisor doing his check at the hour mark, noting the progress without comment, which was the kind of non-comment that meant satisfactory.

Jax answered the questions that got asked and offered nothing additional. This was his standard operating register. Nothing about it was different from any other shift.

The shard began to pulse at the end of the first hour.

Not loudly. Not in any way that Reiss or Dak or the section supervisor could have detected -- no visible light through the ore dust and work clothes, no heat signature, nothing that registered on any instrument he was carrying. Just the quality he had spent eleven years categorizing as *unexplained -- possibly relevant* and one week learning had a more specific name: a directional pressure, faint and consistent, oriented southwest. Toward the approach terrain. Toward the transition zone. Toward the valley that was not on any public chart.

He adjusted his grip on the extraction tool. The adjustment moved his hand to a position that dampened the contact point -- a quarter-turn, slight repositioning of the palm, the kind of micro-correction that looked like nothing to anyone watching. He did not pause the work to make it. He did not think about when he had learned which grip positions dampened the contact and which activated it. He had apparently learned this during the week at the valley without specifically intending to, the way you learned things that you were paying attention to even when you were telling yourself you weren't paying attention.

He worked around the pulse for the rest of the shift. Eight hours. The shard oriented southwest for eight hours and he adjusted his grip and kept his work rhythm and maintained the specific internal stillness of a person who is managing something they have decided not to address. It was, he noted at one point in the sixth hour without letting the note go anywhere, more effort than silence.

He surfaced at shift's end feeling the specific kind of tired that was not physical.

That night the dream came differently.

Not just the face. The face had a room around it now: walls that were not fully visible, a sense of space and scale that the fragment had never had before. The light in the room came from somewhere off to the left, and the quality of it -- warm, specific, belonging to a particular place at a particular hour -- was something he almost recognized before he could stabilize the recognition. And a voice. Not words, not audible in the sense of sounds he could identify -- but the quality of a voice at the very threshold of hearing, the texture of something being said that he was one precise step away from being able to understand. He woke with his hand on the bunk edge and the voice already fading and lay very still, which did not arrest the fading, and lay until the fading completed and he was left with the shape of a room and nothing inside it.

He was more depleted the next morning than he had been the morning before. He had been more depleted each morning since the return. He attributed this to re-acclimation to DE's air quality and the resumed physical demands of shift work, which was a plausible explanation for a man who was accustomed to finding plausible explanations.

He did not write any of this in the journal.

He went to work.

## Scene 3 -- Keeper Quarter Watch

Three days back at DE, and the Keeper Quarter was watching in the way it always watched -- without watching.

He had been taking the evening route past it since his first year at DE, for reasons that had always been straightforwardly practical: it was the most direct path between the mine head and his quarters, and directness was a habit he had no reason to break. He had walked this lane on somewhere between two and three thousand evenings. He had never had occasion to think of it as a choice.

He was thinking about it now. Not with enough force to change the route -- the route was still the shortest path -- but with a new awareness that sitting in the lane was a decision being made in the same way breathing was a decision: continuously, without acknowledgment, with consequences that accumulated invisibly until they didn't.

DE's evening settled around him as he walked. The amber light was doing what it did at this hour, gathering in the particulates and going warm, the quality of it that he had always recognized as beautiful in the way damaged things were sometimes beautiful. The air had cooled from the plateau's afternoon heat. The shift change had mostly completed; the lanes were moving with the specific traffic of people going from one place they had to be to another. The sounds were familiar: equipment, footfall, the distant clank from the secondary smelter, a conversation behind a closed door that he caught a word of and lost.

He turned onto the Keeper Quarter lane.

The residents were in their evening arrangements -- the ones who were outside, outside; the ones who were not, visible occasionally through windows. A woman was tending the window box on the ground floor of the second building, turning the soil with a small tool, adjusting the position of something growing. She was working with the particular unhurried method of a person who was outside for the air and the evening and the incidental advantage of being positioned to see the lane. A man sat on the steps of the fourth building with what looked like a piece of equipment in his hands, doing something to it with a small tool, not looking up. Two younger residents moved between the fifth and sixth buildings with the pace of people who had somewhere to be and weren't in a hurry to get there.

None of them looked at him directly.

He was aware, with a precision he had not had three weeks ago, that this was not ordinary inattention. They had been tracking his return for three days -- had registered it when he walked through the gate, had noted his route resuming its usual pattern, were monitoring the specific question of whether the man who had made the crossing and returned would make it again. The peripheral attention of people who had been watching for a specific outcome for a very long time, and who knew better than to let the watching show.

The man on the steps said, without looking up, without directing it anywhere in particular: "He'll be back soon enough."

Jax's stride did not break. He kept his eyes on the lane and kept walking.

Three questions formed in the space behind his next four steps and did not get asked.

The first was the obvious one: who. He already knew who. The question existed only as a door, and opening the door was confirmation -- of his own identity in this situation, of the role the lane had been assigning him for longer than he'd been aware of it, of the fact that he was the subject of a sentence spoken with that specific certainty. The not-asking was the last available instrument for keeping the door closed. He kept it closed.

The second was harder to avoid: what they knew. The Keeper Quarter residents knew something about his trajectory that he hadn't told them -- something they'd inherited or maintained or received through channels he didn't have full knowledge of. Asking would acknowledge that there was a channel. Acknowledging the channel acknowledged the network. Acknowledging the network acknowledged that what he'd done at the Valley was a point of entry into something, not a visit from which he'd cleanly departed. The walking-away he'd done five days ago was beginning to look, from this angle, less like an exit and more like an interruption.

The third was the one he couldn't approach directly. It was the question about timeline -- the one the man's phrasing had opened and then left standing: *soon enough*. Asking it would require him to speak the word *when*, and speaking *when* required accepting that *if* had already resolved, and accepting that *if* had resolved required accepting the frame that the Keeper Quarter was operating in, the frame that the Valley was operating in, the frame that the shard in its glowing tin box was operating in, the frame that he had been refusing to operate in by the active labor of daily maintenance since he'd returned. He could see the shape of the question from here. He declined to walk toward it.

He kept walking. The lane continued. The amber light did what it always did.

The three questions came with him for the rest of the evening and he did not examine that they were there.

## Scene 4 -- The Journals

He opened the journal before he understood why he'd opened it.

He had come back to his quarters with the specific intention of processing the survey notes he'd left unlogged before the Valley trip -- documentation that represented a backlog he could account for and address, a concrete task with a concrete completion state. He sat at the table. He opened the journal. He was looking at the last entry before the motion of opening had fully registered.

*She said surviving alone isn't the same as living. She's right. I haven't decided what to do about it.*

He looked at this for a while.

He had not decided. He was aware that he had not decided, and that the not-deciding had been accumulating for four days in the specific way of things that occupy space without being acknowledged -- a presence that was taking up the same cognitive real estate that a decision would have taken, requiring the same recurrent attention, surfacing with the same regularity at the end of a shift when the physical work's demand released its hold on the foreground.

The survey notes were in a different pocket of the pack. He had not processed them because processing them would mean opening the log, and opening the log would mean opening this journal, and opening this journal at any point in the last four days would have meant encountering what he had written, and encountering what he had written would mean choosing whether to add to it. He had been choosing not to add to it by not opening the journal. He had now opened the journal without deciding to, which meant the choice had resolved without his formal participation, and he was looking at the entry anyway, and he was still not adding to it.

He closed the journal.

He set it on the table and looked at the shelf across the room.

The tin box had a line of cold light running along the seam where the lid met the base. Thin -- the kind of light that only registered in a room that was dim enough, which this room was, the evening having come in while he was in the Keeper Quarter lane. The shard's glow: cold, blue-white, the same color and steadiness it had found at the Shrine, unchanged by being enclosed, unchanged by the return journey, unchanged by his decision to box it and leave it and not look at it and not think about what it meant that it was glowing through the lid of a closed box.

He looked at it from across the room for a moment.

He had work to do. He knew this was exactly the problem.
