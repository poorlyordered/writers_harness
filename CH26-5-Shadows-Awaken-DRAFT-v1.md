# Chapter 26.5 — Interlude: Cassius Drayton

## Beat 1

The fever has been with him since he was seventeen.

Not a metaphor. A fact he has not been able to separate from himself since the morning it arrived — a Tuesday, the kind of unremarkable day that turns out to have been the last unremarkable day. He had been in his father's study, reading the fragment of Varr history his father had spent thirty years recovering, and something in the text had reached through the page and touched something he had not known was there.

He had not told anyone. He had been seventeen and he had understood, immediately and completely, that this was not something you told anyone.

Twenty-three years later, the fever is still there. It is the most constant thing in his life — more constant than the operation, more constant than the reach and the resources and the two decades of work. He has organized everything around it. He does not think of this as a problem.

He is running through the morning briefing when the first report from the Kessel sector arrives. He sets it aside. He will read it when the briefing is complete. He has learned to finish one thing before moving to the next; the fever has tried to make him the kind of man who doesn't, and he has resisted. This is discipline. He is proud of his discipline.

He finishes the briefing. He reads the report.

---

## Beat 2

The infiltration team was dispersed.

Not destroyed — dispersed. He reads the operational detail with the care of someone who does not accept summaries as substitutes for data. Twelve operatives, well-equipped, moving on a Dissenter relay station eleven sectors from the last confirmed signal cluster. The station was defended, which was expected. It was defended by resources he did not expect, which is the data that matters.

Someone warned them.

He reads this again. The relay station's defenders had advance intelligence about the infiltration team's logistics channel. Someone had been monitoring his operation's secondary communication architecture — the layer below the Stellarium-adjacent encryption his backers supplied. The layer he had believed was secure.

It is not secure.

He sets the report down. He is quiet for a moment in the way he is quiet when he is processing something that requires full attention: the room lowers to meet him. His aide, across the table, understands this quality of silence and does not fill it.

The Dissenters have an intelligence asset he did not know about. An asset sophisticated enough to penetrate his secondary architecture. An asset that has been watching long enough to intercept a logistics channel that has only been in operation for eighteen months.

He has been watched.

He picks up the report again and reads it from the beginning.

---

## Beat 3

He is not angry.

His aide, watching him process this, expects anger. Everyone expects anger when the operation fails, because everyone has seen what happens when he is angry, and they have learned to watch for it. He does not give them what they expect. He never shouts. Volume is for people who have lost.

What he feels is something more precise than anger. The fever is running hotter than it was an hour ago — he can feel it, the specific elevation of something that responds to obstacles the way a current responds to a narrowing channel: faster, more focused, higher pressure through the same material. The obstacle has not stopped the current. It has increased its force.

He draws a line through the secondary communication protocol on the briefing document. He will not use it again. He writes *compromised — retire immediately* in the margin and hands it to his aide, and the aide understands this is a complete communication.

The intelligence asset is a problem. A contained problem. Someone in his operation talked, or someone in his backers' networks talked, or the asset is better than he thought. He will determine which.

What he does not have is the Valley.

The Valley is still the problem. The Valley has been the problem for twenty-three years. Everything else — the operation, the resources, the reach — has been infrastructure built toward the day when its location is known. He is closer than he has ever been. He can feel the closeness.

He picks up the sector map.

---

## Beat 4

The search pattern is a compression.

He has been running it for six weeks — a passive surveillance architecture that eliminates sectors rather than identifies targets, which is slower and less satisfying and more accurate. The fever wants him to move. He does not move. He eliminates.

He looks at what remains.

Eleven sectors eliminated. Three remaining. One of the three contains something — he can feel it the way he has always felt things through the fever, not with the precision of a compass but with the certainty of a body that knows which direction is warm. The Kessel relay station was in a sector adjacent to one of the three remaining. The Dissenters defended it with advance intelligence about his logistics channel. Which means someone in one of the three remaining sectors found out fast enough to warn the relay station.

Someone connected to the relay station's early-warning network.

He stares at the map for a long time.

The Valley is in one of three sectors. And someone there has been watching him for eighteen months, with access to intelligence methods he did not know existed.

He is closer than he has ever been. The fever agrees.

---

## Beat 5

He remembers the first time Stellarium responded to him.

He had been twenty-two. The fragment of Varr text had told him what the fever was — imperfectly, incompletely, but enough: a bloodline signal, a call, the architecture of a civilization his ancestors had been part of. His ancestors had been right. The other faction had sabotaged them. He was the continuation.

He had found a fragment of Stellarium in a trader's collection — a splinter, barely enough to feel. He had held it. It had responded.

Not the way the Varr accounts described the Daybringer interface — the directional warmth, the specific language of orientation. What he felt was different. Larger. Less precise. Like a signal received by an instrument that wasn't built for the frequency: present, powerful, and blurring at the edges in a way that told him the instrument and the signal were not quite matched.

He had assumed the mismatch was training. He had spent twenty-three years trying to resolve it through acquisition, through expansion, through the relentless building of infrastructure that would eventually give him the archive. The archive would give him the framework. The framework would resolve the mismatch.

He has not resolved the mismatch.

He does not think about this. He returns to the map.

---

## Beat 6

He deploys the elite unit at fourteen hundred hours.

Eight operatives. The best his operation has produced across twenty-three years of selecting for capability and loyalty in that order. Equipped with the interface readers his backers supplied — technology that responds to Stellarium-adjacent signal the way a tuning fork responds to its frequency. If the Daybringer is in one of the three remaining sectors, the readers will find the signal.

His aide asks about the operational parameters. He gives them in the order they matter: find the signal, identify the location, hold for direct instruction. Do not engage. Do not escalate. Do not make a decision about confrontation without his explicit authorization.

He says the last part clearly. He has learned that the fever driving him also drives the people he has selected, and people selected for capability do not always exercise the judgment that capability requires. He has seen what the fever's urgency produces without his instruction. He does not permit it.

The unit deploys. He watches them go.

The fever is running hotter than it has run since the early years. He is close. He does not know yet exactly how close — but the feeling is the feeling he has been waiting twenty-three years to feel, and it is here, and he holds it the way he has always held things that matter: tightly, without showing it.

---

## Beat 7

By early evening, the pull is different.

He has felt the Stellarium signal for twenty-three years. He knows its texture the way he knows his own heartbeat — present, constant, the background of everything. He has learned to read its variations: hotter when he moves in the right direction, cooler when he turns away, different again near a Stellarium deposit or adjacent technology.

Tonight it is none of those.

The blurring at the edges — the indication that his instrument and the frequency are not quite matched, the thing he has been trying to resolve for two decades — is less blurred. Not gone. Less. Something in one of the three remaining sectors is producing a signal that resonates with his in a way he has not encountered before. Not Stellarium itself. Something that has been shaped by Stellarium, or that is shaping it, in a configuration that brings the frequency closer to his reception range.

He sits with this for a long time.

Someone in one of the three remaining sectors is doing something with Stellarium that is changing the signal.

Someone with capability. Someone trained.

He has been looking for the archive. He has found, instead, the thing the archive was built to produce.

---

## Beat 8

He knows which direction.

Not the sector — the sectors are a grid, a tool, a way of thinking about the problem at search scale. This is not grid-scale knowing. This is the fever knowing, the body knowing, the specific certainty of someone tuned to a frequency for twenty-three years who has just heard it resolve from noise into signal.

Southwest. The third remaining sector. Something in the southwest quadrant, at a distance he cannot yet measure precisely, is producing a signal his instrument can receive more clearly than it has ever received anything.

He does not celebrate. Celebration is not the quality of this moment. The quality of this moment is the specific stillness of someone who has been moving toward a thing for a very long time and has just seen it clearly enough to move with precision rather than hope.

He opens the sector map. He marks the quadrant.

He has been right for twenty-three years — right about the bloodline, the archive, the direction of the pull, the injustice that demanded a response. He has built everything on the certainty that he was right. The certainty has never wavered. It does not waver now.

He does not know that the signal he is following belongs to the man who will defeat him.

He is moving anyway.
