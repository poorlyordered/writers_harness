# Chapter 16

## Scene 1 — The Incursion

Quill said it quietly, while they were managing the fuel resupply logistics at the transit stop's dock, and said it the way he said things that mattered — not urgently, not loudly, just with the specific weight of information that needed to pass between two people without announcing itself to the room.

"Maintenance access, upper dock. Three o'clock. He's been there since we docked."

Jax did not look. He continued his argument with the fueling crew about the transit fee, which was slightly higher than the posted rate and required exactly the kind of negotiation that looked like routine routine. "And."

"Second one in the loading bay behind us. Came in from a different direction than the other dock workers. Arrived after we did." A beat. "Someone knew we'd be here. The third one I don't have a location on yet."

Jax finished the negotiation, paid the adjusted fee, and turned to supervise the loading of a supply container — normal logistics, nothing that needed attention. While he did, he read the dock the way he read an unfamiliar shaft: angles, load-bearing points, what the architecture gave you and what it took away.

The upper dock position was elevated — good for observation, slow for movement. The loading bay was a chokepoint if you didn't know it and a kill zone if you did. Between those two positions and the team's ship at the dock: two approach angles. The third operative was probably covering the line to the ship at ground level.

He moved toward Mira, not stopping: "Upper dock, loading bay, ground-level unknown. Thirty seconds."

She nodded once. Already revising.

He touched the inside of his jacket. The shard was warm — warmer than transit neutral, the orientation quality he knew from before the attack on DE. Not pulling him anywhere. Running hotter than usual. Present in the way of something paying attention.

He kept moving.

## Scene 2 — The Defense

The ground-level operative moved first, which told Jax he was the least patient of the three and probably the most experienced — the kind of professional who had learned that waiting cost more than acting.

He came out of a service alcove at Jax's nine o'clock, which was exactly where Jax would have positioned himself to cover the ship's access ramp from ground level. The position was correct. The timing was slightly off — thirty seconds before his upper-dock counterpart had repositioned, which opened a gap in the flanking move.

Jax read this in the same moment he read the dock's material. Composite paneling on the walls. Load-bearing columns at ten-meter intervals. Fourteen meters of open space between the service alcove and the ship's ramp. He knew from two trials and one failure what he could do to fourteen meters of nothing if the intention was set before the channel opened.

He set the intention. Held the shape of it — specific, bounded, the column and not the man.

The shard came up fast and hot against his sternum. Faster than any training session. More responsive, as if the live context had unlocked something that controlled conditions had been damping.

He let it go. It went where he pointed.

Not perfectly — drift, maybe fifteen degrees off the intended vector — but he had read the column's density before he'd released anything and he knew what composite did when force hit it at that angle. The output deflected off the column and spread into the alcove space, not the man. The operative went down behind the column, sheltered by it, untouched — but not advancing.

The miner's eye had done the work. He'd looked at the column the way he looked at rock, known what would happen when force met it, and aimed at the structure rather than the person. The physics did the rest.

Aria-7, from the ship's comms — she'd kept a channel open without being asked: "Upper dock target moving to ladder access. Ground level in twenty seconds."

Quill was already at the loading bay entrance — not in front of it, positioned in the gap between two cargo containers with a sightline inside but no profile visible from within. He looked at Jax and held up two fingers.

Two in the bay. Not one.

Ground-level operative pinned. Upper-dock descending. Two in the bay. Mira somewhere at his back.

From the dock: "Jax." Toby's voice. Just that.

He turned. Toby had repositioned the ship's loading ramp — not extended it, but angled the boarding configuration so the port thruster array faced the open dock space at a low angle. Not a weapon. A wall, if he used it right.

"Start the pre-ignition sequence," Jax said.

Toby did it. The port thrusters wound up — rising whine, a wash of heat across the dock surface — and the displacement pushed anyone in the open space toward the walls. The upper-dock operative froze on the ladder rungs. The ground-level operative stayed behind the column.

The loading bay: two people about to discover whether the bay's exhaust rating matched a full thruster startup.

It didn't.

Both operatives came out the front of the bay fast, into the dock space, directly into Mira's field of view. She said two words to them. They stopped. Whatever they read in her was apparently accurate.

The upper-dock operative descended the ladder and walked toward the far end of the dock at a pace that said *this is not retreat, this is choosing to leave.*

The shard was still hot at his sternum. Not cold — hot. Different from the training sessions' immediate drain. The thermal exchange was running, but slowly, as if the output had been sustainable rather than total. He noted this and filed it.

## Scene 3 — After

Mira's perimeter assessment was complete before anyone else had stopped moving. She walked the dock in the pattern that covered the primary approach angles, checked the loading bay, confirmed the upper access was clear, and returned with the verdict: "They're gone. No secondary team. This was a probe."

"Looking for our position," Quill said. "They found it."

"And left with it," Aria-7 said from comms. "Which means this stop is no longer useful as a waypoint. I recommend departing before they can relay our position with sufficient specificity for a follow-on."

Toby was already running the departure sequence. "Agreed. Also the pre-ignition voided the fueling contract, so we're at sixty percent capacity and I'd rather not be here when the dock manager works that out."

They boarded. The ship cleared the dock with the efficiency of a crew that had done this before and had clear reasons for speed.

In the cabin, Jax sat and let the shard run its restore. The warmth in his chest was different from the training sessions' hollow ache — less absence, more spent weight, the feeling of having done real work rather than failed work. He pressed his palm flat against his sternum and felt the exchange completing and let it.

He was aware of the team in his peripheral attention in a way that was different from before.

Aria-7, reviewing the engagement data at her panel: "The ability's output was directionally accurate within fifteen degrees. The column deflection was within predicted parameters. The thermal signature indicates the interface remained in a sustainable output range — it did not approach the overload threshold." She said this with her characteristic even delivery. Then: "The geological application was effective."

He looked at her. She was looking at her panel. She had named it the same way she named everything — technical, precise — but she had named it. He didn't examine why that registered the way it did.

Quill, running the replacement sensor cluster through its checks without looking up: "For the record, the column read was correct. I would not have thought of the column."

Nobody responded. It sat in the cabin air with the quality of something true that everyone had clocked and nobody was going to make ceremonial.

Toby, from the cockpit, with a careful casualness that suggested the casualness was doing some work: "New heading is set. Four-hour detour but it's cleaner." He didn't look back.

Mira was at her position, watching the nav display with the patient attention of someone taking a new measurement and revising her model accordingly. She said nothing. She had said almost nothing since the dock. With Mira, that was its own kind of remark.

Jax let it all settle around him. He didn't address any of it directly. He was watching the dock recede on the rear display, checking the shard's temperature against his sternum, thinking about the next leg of the route.

They had seen it work. He had felt it work.

The emotion-intention problem wasn't solved — the drift was still there, fifteen degrees, and he had compensated with the column rather than corrected the underlying issue. But the compensation had been right. He had read the space and used what he had where it was useful. The miner's eye and the ability and the geometry of the situation had worked together in a way that the training sessions had been trying to reach and hadn't.

He had done that. He could do that.

He had this.
