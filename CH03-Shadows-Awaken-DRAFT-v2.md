# Chapter 3 -- Link to Antagonist

## Scene 1 -- News Moves Through the Colony

The news came through Okafor's the way most things came through Okafor's: without announcement, carried in pieces by three different people who each had a fraction of it, assembled by whoever was paying attention when the pieces arrived.

Jax was on his second cup of canteen-grade coffee, standing at the counter near the door the way he always stood, when the first piece came in. A convoy worker off an overnight corridor run -- young, still in his transit gear, the specific look of someone who had been awake for too many hours and was processing what he'd heard during them. He settled at the table behind Jax and started talking to the person already there: a mining crew three sectors over hadn't reported. Standard operation, no outstanding safety flags. Two comms cycles of silence.

Jax didn't turn around. He listened.

The man at the table asked the obvious questions. The convoy worker answered them the way someone answered questions they'd already been over several times in their head: briefly, with the impatience of a person whose own processing had moved past the obvious into something they hadn't resolved yet. Last recorded contact was routine. No distress signal. Equipment had pinged at its six-hour interval and then stopped pinging. The convoy worker had heard this from the transit hub dispatcher, who had heard it from the claim operator's emergency contact, which was now two removes from the source and losing resolution at each step.

The second piece came two hours later, in the equipment bay at the locker station. The man running the adjacent bank was relaying something he'd heard from the night shift foreman about the same situation, with a different detail attached: someone had pulled the last comms log from the missing crew's equipment. Nothing unusual in it. A scan result. A minor equipment flag, the kind that showed up three times a shift and got noted and ignored. A weather visibility note. And then the log ended, cleanly, the way a log ended when the equipment stopped rather than when the operator signed off.

The man at the adjacent locker bank said the word "fast" twice. The first time as description. The second time because the first time hadn't contained everything he meant by it.

Jax checked his scanner charge and said nothing.

The third piece was at the shift assignment queue, and this piece was different from the first two because it came with witnesses rather than reports. Two workers had transferred in overnight from a smaller extraction operation -- independent outfit, positioned one sector closer to the missing crew than DE was. They were quiet. Not the quiet of people who had nothing to say; the specific quiet of people who had decided what they were and weren't going to say and were holding the line on it. One of them, a woman with the look of someone who had been in extraction work for a long time and had seen things go wrong before, said when pressed by the person in front of her in line: it had not looked like an accident. It had looked like someone who knew what they were doing and had done it before and had not left anything behind them that explained what had happened.

What it had looked like beyond that she didn't say. Nobody pressed her for it. The line moved forward.

Jax took his assignment and went to work.

He had three pieces now. He ran through them the way he ran through survey data: not rushing toward a conclusion, building the picture from what was actually in it. A crew gone silent with no warning, no distress call, no mechanical explanation in the final log. Equipment that pinged on schedule and stopped pinging. A competitor's operation shut down -- not abandoned, not failed: shut down -- in a way that left one of its workers unable to describe what she had seen without deciding not to describe it. Workers arriving at DE not because DE was better but because whatever had happened made staying the less tolerable option.

Someone was moving through the region. Moving with purpose and in a way that knew what it was doing and had resources behind it and did not appear to be concerned about what it left behind. The scale of that was legible. The shape was legible.

What he didn't have yet was the reason underneath it: what someone would want badly enough from this region to work it this way.

He filed the absence of an answer and went into the mine.

## Scene 2 -- The Shard Pulls

His assignment took him into a sector he'd run twice before: a lateral extension off the eastern survey corridor, close enough to the missing crew's last-known position that on a quiet day he could have flagged their operation on his scanner's regional display.

He checked. The flag wasn't there.

He put the scanner to work.

He'd put the shard in his pocket that morning without examining why. It had stayed in his hand longer than usual when he was packing his kit, and when he'd closed the tin he'd slipped it into his pocket rather than back inside. He'd noted this and let it stand. Eleven years of doing what the shard seemed to ask for, including the morning it had seemed to ask to travel closer to him than the tin box allowed.

The shift was methodical. Two stress concentrations in the upper face, an interesting lateral stratification worth flagging for follow-up. The work required enough attention to keep the mind from wandering. He let it require that.

Midway through a pass along the eastern wall, the shard pulsed.

Not a hum. Not the sustained low frequency from the past two mornings. This was shorter and more forceful -- a single beat through his palm and up his wrist before he'd registered what it was. He went still.

The shard pulsed again.

He took it out of his pocket and held it flat on his palm. The shaft around him was unchanged: pale fractured geology, the distant conveyors, the regular mechanical register of an operation running the way it was supposed to run. His scanner registered nothing anomalous.

A third pulse. Oriented -- pointed at something specific, the same directional quality he'd felt in his quarters, but stronger now. He turned slowly, keeping his hand level. The pulse strengthened as he faced northeast. Toward the sector where the crew had last reported.

He stood there for a moment.

He knew what the sensible response was. He had work to do. He had a survey pass half-finished and annotations to log and a stratification worth flagging. The sensible response was to put the shard back in his pocket, pick up his scanner, and complete the pass. He had done the sensible thing with the shard for eleven years. He had filed the inexplicable and moved on from it and done the work in front of him, and eleven years of that had left him with good survey logs and a piece of mineral he could not explain and a dream that had just shown him a room he didn't recognize.

He picked up his tools and went northeast.

## Scene 3 -- What the Shard Found

He told himself he was checking the boundary. Verifying the adjacent sector matched his regional map, confirming no geological overlap with his own survey area. This was not entirely a lie. A miner who understood neighboring formations did better work in his own section. He had used this reasoning before, in year four and year seven, when the shard had pulled him off his assigned path and toward something he couldn't name. Both times he'd found nothing. Both times he'd returned to his work with the specific clean feeling of having checked and been wrong, which was its own kind of data.

He went northeast for ten minutes before he stopped telling himself anything.

The shard had gone from pulsing to something more continuous -- not quite the sustained hum from his quarters, but a pressure that came in fast, close intervals, rising as he moved. He kept his hand level and followed it the way he'd learned to follow a signal: not chasing, not forcing, just keeping the direction and letting the distance close.

The geology changed as he moved. The pale fractured rock of his own sector gave way to something older and less worked: darker stone, stress fractures running in a different pattern, surfaces that hadn't seen scanner light recently. He was past the boundary of his assigned area. He was in someone else's claim. At the edge of his lantern's reach he could make out survey markers from another operation -- a flag on a stake, a painted boundary indicator, the small signs that said someone had been here, was working here, had not been here recently. He kept moving.

The shard pulsed once more, strong enough that he felt it in his teeth, the oscillation traveling up through his wrist and into his jaw before it resolved.

Then it went quiet.

He stopped.

She was at the far edge of the lantern light. Or she was present there -- the distinction mattered in a way he didn't have words for yet, the difference between something standing somewhere and something existing at the edge of where the light reached. Small. White hair loose around her shoulders, not pinned or braided, the way someone wore their hair when they were somewhere they considered private. He had the impression of age without the appearance of frailty; the posture was settled in a way that had nothing to do with stillness and everything to do with someone who had arrived ahead of the situation and was simply waiting for it to catch up.

She was looking at him.

Not at the space around him. Not past him. At him, with the specific direct quality of someone who had been looking for something for a very long time and had now located it, and was neither surprised nor urgent about this, because they had always known the locating would happen. There was recognition in it. Not warmth exactly -- or warmth was there but underneath something older and more settled, the way bedrock was underneath productive geology. She had found him. She was not going to do anything dramatic about it.

She didn't speak. She didn't move toward him.

She was translucent in the way nothing solid was ever translucent -- the fractured rock showed through her, the survey markers, the geometry of the shaft continuing into darkness behind her shape. But she was not a trick of the light. He had spent eleven years in mines reading the things light did to surfaces and to his own tired eyes, and he knew the inventory of what his brain produced under various conditions of fatigue and pressure, and this was not in that inventory. She was there. She was looking at him. Something in his chest had moved from its ordinary position and was now registering this as real in the way that very few things registered as real before the reasoning mind could mount a counterargument.

He took a step back.

She remained. Still looking. The patience of her was the most unsettling thing about it -- not the translucence, not the impossibility of her being in this shaft, but the way she was prepared to wait as long as this took, because she had already waited longer than this and was not in any hurry.

He took another step back. Then another. Then he turned and walked the way he'd come at a pace that was not quite a run, because a run would have required him to acknowledge what he was running from, and he was not ready to do that.

He returned to his survey area. He picked up his scanner. He logged the remaining concentrations, completed his passes, flagged the stratification for follow-up, and came out of the mine when the shift-end signal ran: three long pulses, one short.

He did not write it in his kit notes. He put the shard back in his pocket on the walk out and kept his eyes on the path ahead and his hands occupied with the work of returning equipment and did not look northeast.

## Scene 4 -- The Look He Recognized

Heliosanthus was low on the horizon when he came out. The dust in the air caught the light and turned everything amber: the mine head structures, the ore-processing towers, the long column of end-of-shift workers walking the corridor back toward the residential blocks. He joined them and let the rhythm carry him. His body knew this route well enough that he could follow it without directing it, which meant his attention was elsewhere, going back over the shaft and the lantern light and the quality of the patience in that look, trying to fit it into any category he had and finding that none of them fit.

He was three blocks from the residential section when he passed the Keeper Quarter.

At this hour the cluster of older buildings had its usual quality: stillness that persisted even when the rest of DE was in motion. He had passed this cluster enough times to have stopped noticing it and started noticing it again, in the way that recurring things occasionally surfaced from habit into sight. He was not thinking about it consciously when he came level with it.

An older woman was outside, working on the door frame of the building nearest the street. Repair work: a sealant tool in one hand, a length of gasket material on the step beside her. Maintenance that could be done at any hour, being done at this one. She had the deliberate quality of someone who was not in a hurry and never had been. She heard him coming -- or knew he was there by some mechanism that didn't require hearing -- and looked up.

She held the look for a moment. Not a greeting, not a challenge. Something quieter than either, with an attentiveness underneath it that was out of proportion to the task she'd been doing, the way certain people's full attention arrived before they'd decided to give it.

Then she returned to her work.

He kept walking.

He was half a block past when the connection arrived. Not the look itself but the quality of it -- the directness, the unhurried weight of being seen by someone who did not need anything from him but had registered his presence as significant. The sense that he was being witnessed by someone who already knew more than the moment contained.

He had been on the receiving end of that quality once already today.

He stopped walking. The end-of-shift current moved around him without comment. He stood in it and let the comparison complete itself: a woman at the edge of lantern light in a dark shaft, present in a way that defied the physics of presence. A woman at the edge of the Keeper Quarter in amber end-of-shift light, doing repair work on a door frame. The quality of the attention was the same. Not the face, not the age, not anything physically identical. The specific weight of being seen by someone who had been watching longer than you knew.

His pulse did something he hadn't asked it to do.

Then he moved again. His quarters. The tin box on the shelf. He opened it, placed the shard inside, and closed it. The shard was quiet, the way it went quiet after it had done whatever it had done. He sat on the edge of his cot for about thirty seconds.

He had work to do tomorrow. That was the relevant fact.

He did not look back at the Quarter when he'd passed it. He did not open the tin box again that night.
