## Scene 2 -- The Theme Spoken

He was running a lateral extension off the eastern survey corridor when Petersen made his comment.

Petersen was one of the fixtures of Heliosanthus Mines, and fixtures of Heliosanthus Mines were rarer than the ore itself. Most miners came to DE on contract -- one year, three years, long enough to stake something or give up staking and leave. Petersen had come at twenty-six and was now in his mid-fifties, which meant he had been here nearly three decades, which meant DE had shaped itself around his presence rather than the other way around. He knew the geological formations the way Jax knew them -- not from charts but from contact, from years of the rock talking back. He had the specific quality of someone who had arrived young, decided to stay, and stopped dividing the world into past and future somewhere around the ten-year mark. Not unhappy. Not broken by the place. Settled in a way that was complete enough to look like peace and, in certain lights, troubling enough to look like something else.

They'd worked adjacent sections dozens of times across the years. They talked about the geology when they needed to and not otherwise. Jax preferred it that way. Petersen had learned to.

They'd been at it for two hours, running parallel survey passes through a formation the automated systems had flagged as potentially stratified. The work was methodical and required enough attention to keep the mind from wandering. Good work, the kind that could be trusted to fill a shift without asking anything extra from the person doing it. Jax was logging a stress concentration in the upper face when Petersen spoke.

"Nobody finds Stellarium," Petersen said. Not to Jax specifically. Somewhere between himself and the rock. "It finds them."

Jax marked the concentration and moved his scanner eighteen centimeters.

"That's religion talking," he said. "Stellarium is a mineral. It's in the ground somewhere and I'm going to find it."

Petersen shrugged. The shrug covered everything: agreement, disagreement, the fundamental indifference of a man who had made his peace with what the ground did and didn't give up. He went back to his pass. Jax went back to his.

The exchange lasted eight seconds. Neither of them thought much of it afterward.

Jax thought about it afterward.

Not the content of it -- the content was familiar, the same comfortable mysticism that ran through DE's long-timer population the way mineral particulate ran through their lungs: invisible, accumulated, mostly unexamined. He'd heard versions of the same idea across eleven years, from people who'd given up looking and needed a framework that made the giving-up feel like wisdom. Stellarium drawn to a person, a person drawn to Stellarium, some cosmic arrangement that put the right mineral in the hands of the right finder. He'd dismissed it in year one for the same reason he dismissed it now: it was comfortable thinking. It made the search feel inevitable rather than effortful. It let a person stop looking because they trusted the finding would come on its own schedule.

What he was thinking about was Petersen's thirty years. The man had been here twice as long as Jax. He knew the formations, ran his passes correctly, kept his equipment in good repair. He was, by any professional measure, a competent miner. And somewhere in those thirty years he had settled into the conviction that the finding was not his to force. That patience was the skill. That whatever the ground had for him, it would give when it was ready.

Jax did not stop looking. He had not stopped for eleven years. Whatever Stellarium did or didn't do, it wasn't going to find him sitting still. But he moved the scanner and kept working with the specific small weight of someone who has seen one version of what he doesn't want to become, and found it looks like contentment from the outside.
