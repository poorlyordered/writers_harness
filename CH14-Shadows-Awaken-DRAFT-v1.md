# Chapter 14

## Scene 1 — The Protocol

The comms signal had a quarter-second delay that Nalani's voice had learned to work around — she paused at the end of sentences in a way she didn't when they spoke in person, giving the transmission time to arrive before she continued. It wasn't much. It was enough to make the conversation feel like a different thing than it had been at the Shrine. Guidance at a distance had a different texture. He was getting used to it.

"The ability as it appeared in the attack on DE was not a targeted expression," she said. "You know this. What happened was a flare — omnidirectional, consuming the available capacity of the interface without directing it anywhere specific. If that happens in a confined space, the result is not a weapon. It's a catastrophe."

He knew. He had been thinking about it since they left.

"What we're working toward is a directed expression. Focused. A specific output along a specific vector. The Varr records on this are clear on what the trained form looks like." A pause — the signal lag, or the weight of what she was about to say. "They are less clear on what the training feels like. That will be yours to find."

"I understand," he said.

She went quiet in a way that meant she was satisfied. Or was choosing to be.

Aria-7 had spread the training protocol across the ship's main table in three organized sections: historical record, physical parameters, and what she had labeled, without apparent irony, *empirical calibration sequence*. She stood at the far edge of the table in the way she stood everywhere — present, attentive, making no unnecessary movement.

"The Varr training records document four Daybringer development protocols," she said. "Three are incomplete. One produced what the records describe as a functional result in forty-two sessions. I have adapted the structural elements and removed the components that required facilities we don't have." She indicated the center section of the protocol. "The initial trial uses a controlled environment and a specified target." She placed a small cylinder of structural material — gray, dense, the kind of component used in ship partition walls — at the table's far end. "Three centimeters diameter. Twelve centimeters length. Mass: approximately ninety grams."

He looked at the cylinder. Then at the space between it and where he would stand. Then at the protocol sections she had prepared.

"How far is the target at full trial distance."

"Seven meters. This first trial uses two meters."

He nodded. He didn't touch the protocol. He had what he needed from it.

She watched him not touch it. Something in her expression registered this — the brief recalibration of someone who had prepared a document in order to be useful and was now parsing what it meant that the document wasn't going to be used the way she'd intended. She said nothing.

He moved to the starting position.

## Scene 2 — The Trial

The ability was there.

He had known it would be — it had been there in the attack on DE, had been there in the years of shard-warmth and direction-sense before he had any name for what it was. But knowing it was there and reaching for it deliberately were different operations, and the second one was the foreign one. Like finding a control in a piece of equipment you'd never run. The interface was real; the familiarity wasn't.

He reached. The shard responded — warmth along the inside of his arm, a pressure that wasn't quite pressure, the quality of something building that didn't have the right container yet. He held it for a moment and felt the shape of the thing he was supposed to be directing. Real. With volume and weight and what he could only describe as attitude — a direction it wanted to go, which wasn't necessarily the direction he was pointing at.

He let it out.

The result took out the cylinder and both walls on either side of it and the air duct above the cylinder's position. The structural material held; the duct didn't. A sound like a pressure release, then the secondary sound of something that had been under tension suddenly not being. The cylinder was fine. Everything around it wasn't.

From somewhere behind him, he heard Toby say, very quietly: "Right."

The shard had gone cold the moment the output left him. Not the comfortable cool of its neutral state — cold the way a hand goes cold when it gives everything to keep the rest of the body warm. He felt the hollow in his chest where the energy had been and felt the shard already drawing from him to begin restoring it: slow, steady, the thermal exchange that now had a name. He let it.

He stood in the cleared space and looked at what he'd done.

Too large. Approximately correct — it had gone in the right direction, reached the target, been a focused expression rather than an omnidirectional burst. But the margin of error was the size of a significant portion of the ship, and what he was supposed to be training toward was precision.

Nalani's voice came from the comms station — she had kept the channel open. "What did you feel in the moment before it released."

"Pressure," he said. "Like the shape of the thing outran the shape I was trying to make."

"That's the emotion-intention problem," she said. "Intention points. Emotion powers. If the emotion outpaces the intention, the output is too large. What were you feeling."

He looked at the ruined air duct. This was the question he had least interest in answering. "Forward," he said. Which wasn't quite accurate but was the truest word he had for it.

A silence that felt like she was deciding whether to press it. She decided not to. "The answer is to hold the intention so steadily that the emotion cannot overrun it. The way you hold a bearing in difficult rock — you don't stop feeling the weight. You keep the line."

He heard that. He filed it. He did not ask follow-up questions.

He reset. His stance, the output path in his mind, the thing he was reaching for — he found it again and reached more carefully. The shard warmed slower this time, as if rationing given the draw he'd put through it. He held the warmth and tried to hold the shape: the diameter of the cylinder and nothing beyond it. Smaller. Specific. The thing without what was behind it.

The second attempt went through the cylinder at the correct scale. It went through the table as well, and the section of bulkhead behind it, and the ship's partition wall behind that, which now had a hole in it that Toby was going to have to assess for structural significance.

The cylinder was intact.

He looked at it. The hole in the bulkhead was precise. The aim had been off by something he would later learn was forty-one degrees.

Quill, who had appeared in the doorway at some point Jax hadn't tracked, was looking at the hole with the professional attention of someone calculating repair requirements. He said nothing. He moved to the bulkhead, examined the edges of the damage, and began working through what needed to happen next without being asked.

The shard was colder now. The hollow ache was deeper — not pain exactly, but the kind of absence that occupies space. He felt the slow pull of the thermal exchange and let it.

"Output volume was correct within six percent," Aria-7 said. "Trajectory deviation: forty-one degrees horizontal. The target registered contact. The trial has produced a positive result on the mass parameter."

He did not say thank you. He said, "The deviation was forty-one degrees."

"Yes."

"That's the next problem."

"That's the next problem," she agreed.

From the comms: "Do you want to attempt a third trial today."

He considered this honestly. The shard was still restoring. The ache in his chest was not debilitating, but it was present in the way of something that would be worse if he pushed through it. He had learned enough on DE about what working past the warning signs produced to know when the warning signs were accurate information rather than weakness.

"No," he said.

"Good," Nalani said. No commentary attached to it. The quality of someone who had expected that answer and was relieved by it.

## Scene 3 — After

He cleaned up from the trial the way he cleaned up from any difficult job — methodically, in order, making sure the most structurally significant damage was assessed before the cosmetic. The air duct was the priority; Toby confirmed within ten minutes that the damage was to a secondary line with a redundant path, which meant they had airflow and the duct could wait until the next stop. The bulkhead breach was Quill's assessment. His verdict: structural integrity uncompromised, cosmetic repair possible with materials already aboard, three hours of work he would start now because he wanted to understand the nature of the damage.

Jax let him.

He gathered the cylinder and set it aside — undamaged, which was absurd — and organized Aria-7's protocol sections into a single stack at the corner of the table. He did not read through it. He didn't need to.

Mira had been at the doorway since the second attempt. She said nothing. She watched him work through the cleanup with the patient attention of someone revising a model in light of new data. When he had finished the most immediate of it and was standing in the cleared space with nothing left to put away, she looked at him once, held it a moment, and went back to her position at the secondary entrance.

That was enough.

He sat at the edge of the cleared area with the cylinder in his hand — not examining it, holding the weight of it. The shard was warmer now than it had been at the end of the second attempt. Still not at its usual register, but climbing. He could feel the exchange slowing as the restore approached completion.

*What were you feeling.* He had said *forward.* He'd meant something closer to: eleven years of the same direction, the pull that had been in his inside pocket since he woke up with nothing, the conviction that had always been more reliable than any of the rest of him. That was the emotion. It had outrun the intention because it was larger than the intention — larger than any target he could put it to — and the trial had asked him to pour it through a ninety-gram cylinder at two meters.

He turned it over. The second attempt had been sized right. After Nalani's guidance, the quality of attention had been different — he'd held the shape of the thing more specifically instead of simply reaching, and the emotion-intention ratio had been closer to correct. The aim had been off.

Two separate problems. He had partially solved one. The other was its own operation.

He thought about the forty-one degrees. He was beginning to understand what had happened: he had been so focused on not repeating the volume failure that by the time the thing was shaped correctly, he had stopped attending to where it was pointed. He couldn't hold both at once yet.

He put the cylinder down. He didn't get out the journal. There was nothing to write that he didn't already know, and writing it would have been a different kind of work than the work he actually needed to do, which was to sit with the problem and let it settle into something actionable.

Aria-7 appeared at the edge of the cleared space.

"I've compiled the trial data," she said. "If you want the full summary now or prefer it later."

"Now."

She ran through it. Systematic, complete, precise in a way that missed nothing and prioritized everything correctly. She identified the correct-volume result in the second attempt and noted the specific parameters where his output had been accurate — not as praise, simply as data, the way data is delivered when the goal is accuracy rather than encouragement. She identified the trajectory deviation and noted the probable cause based on her observation of where his attention had shifted between the first and second attempts.

That last part was the most useful thing anyone had said to him all day.

He looked at her. "You saw that."

"I observed the shift in your focus approximately one point three seconds before the output released. It was visible in the shard's thermal signature — the resonance pattern changed slightly before the trajectory deviation."

She had been watching the shard's thermal signature.

He sat with that for a moment. The ache in his chest was almost gone. The shard was warm at his sternum in its ordinary resting register, the thermal exchange complete.

"Thank you," he said.

Aria-7 received this. Something in her expression registered it — the small recalibration of someone who had not fully calculated that their data delivery would land that way. She said, "I'll have the compiled protocol updated before the second trial," and returned to her station.

He sat with what she'd said about the resonance pattern. He turned it once and put it away for the night in the specific way of a problem that needed time to settle before it would be ready to be worked.

Tomorrow: the second trial.
