# Chapter 6 -- Inciting Incident

## Scene 1 -- The Dream Erupts

The dream was always the same: flash, pain, nothing. He had catalogued it for eleven years.

This time it was not the same.

The flash came -- the blinding white he had long since stopped bracing for, the spike of sensation that preceded the nothing. But the nothing did not come. What came instead was the room.

He had glimpsed it before: amber light, gathered rather than diffused, coming from no source he could identify; a surface at chest height, edge cut precisely, catching the light in a way that implied intention. Now it was more solid. The table had things on it -- instruments, containers, equipment whose purpose he recognized in the dream without being able to name when he was awake. The light came from somewhere behind the table, warm and steady, and the air in the room had the quality of a place that had been working: specific, occupied, carrying the residue of effort.

Someone was in the room.

He knew this before he saw her. The presence of another person in the space -- the displacement of attention, the sense that the light fell differently because someone else was inside it. He turned -- no, the dream turned, not him, because in this version he was witnessing rather than moving -- and there was a figure, and then there was a face.

He held it for one fraction of a second.

Dark hair. Eyes that were brown but the specific brown of something that had seen more than it expected and was still looking anyway. A face that was specific in the way only real faces were specific -- belonging to a person rather than a category of person, particular, individual, carrying the weight of itself. He held it.

The nothing took it.

He woke with his hands gripping the bunk's frame, fingers white on the metal edge, his body in the posture of someone who had just tried to hold onto something and failed. The quarters were his quarters, dark except for the dim exterior light from the narrow window. He was in his bunk. He had been in his bunk. The room with the amber light was gone and the face was gone and his hands were gripping metal in the specific way of a person who had closed on air.

He lay still.

He did not get up. He did not open the tin box. He did not write in his journal. The standard protocol after the dream was to get up, do the routine, let the physical work carry the residue away. He lay still and stared at the ceiling in the dark and waited for what he was feeling to move into a category he could work with.

It did not.

He had had the dream for eleven years. He knew its texture the way you knew the texture of a recurring injury: the specific edges of the flash, the quality of the nothing, the aftermath that felt like loss without the content of loss -- the shape of a wound you could feel but not see. He had catalogued it. Written it. Paid for professional interpretation in year five and gotten the same nothing back, categorized differently. The dream was the dream. It was not a window. It was a wall.

This was not a wall. This was the other side of a wall he had been pressing against for eleven years without knowing there was an other side.

He lay there until the light outside changed from nothing to gray, and then to the first amber of Heliosanthus clearing the plateau's eastern edge. He did not close his eyes again.

At some point he understood that what he was waiting for was not going to come. The feeling was not going to recede to a manageable distance. It was not grief -- you could not grieve what you never consciously had. It was something without a clean name: the specific ache of something taken before you knew you were holding it. He had been holding something in that dream, in that fraction of a second, and the nothing had taken it the way the nothing always took everything, and he was left with the shape of it and nothing to put the shape around.

The gray became amber. He got up.

## Scene 2 -- The Shard Glows

He did not open the tin box while he dressed. He opened it when he was ready to leave -- the standard order, every morning: dressed, tools set, tin box checked. He opened it the way he opened it every morning, without expectation, without ceremony, because after eleven years the ceremony had compressed into the action and the expectation had compressed into nothing.

The shard was glowing.

He stood with the tin open and looked at it for what was probably thirty seconds but felt longer. Not a pulse. Not the warmth from the projection. Light -- actual light, coming from inside the mineral, cold and steady, the specific quality of something that was not fire and was not electricity but had been produced by the stone itself from some internal process that had no name in the field guides he'd read in year two when he first tried to understand what he was carrying.

Cold light. Steady. The shard resting in the tin with the locket and the note, the light coming off it in the quiet of his quarters in the early morning like a fact that did not care whether he had a category for it.

He picked it up.

The glow intensified slightly in his hand. Not dramatically -- a degree, a shift in brightness the way a lamp shifts when the power supply stabilizes. He held it. The light continued, steady and cold, and the shard was faintly warm, not the warmth of the projection but a residual warmth, the warmth of something that had been running at a level he hadn't been able to measure.

He set it back down.

The glow continued. Slower when he wasn't touching it -- not slower exactly, but less concentrated, the light spreading differently rather than diminishing. He watched it for another ten seconds. He closed the tin.

He stood in his quarters without deciding anything. The tin was on the shelf. Outside, the first shift was starting -- he could hear the mine head infrastructure cycling up, the conveyors beginning their morning rhythm, the sounds he had stopped hearing years ago and now heard again because he was standing still when he was supposed to be moving.

He took the tin off the shelf.

He opened it. He took out the shard. Warm in his hand, glowing with the cold steady light, a piece of mineral he had carried for eleven years and could not explain and had filed under *unexplained -- not actively investigated* so many times the category had become its home. The same piece of mineral that had been warm once before, in this room, in the afternoon, with a woman standing between the desk and the window telling him he was needed.

He put it in his pocket.

He went to work with the shard glowing faintly through the fabric of his jacket, warm against his hip. He did not check it on the walk to the mine head. He clocked in at the standard time and descended into the northeastern shafts and worked, because working was what he had to do and the shard being what it was did not change what he had to do.

He checked it once, during the first break, in a section of the lateral bore where no one else was working. Still glowing. Still warm. He put it back and continued the survey.

## Scene 3 -- Nalani's Second Appearance

Two hours into the second survey block, the shard pulse-brightened.

Not a sustained rhythm -- a single intensification, sharp and immediate, the kind of signal that arrived before you could decide what to do about it. He was running a scanner pass along the eastern wall of the lateral bore, working solo, and the light from his pocket went from warm-steady to something visible even through the fabric, a brief flare that would have been unmistakable to anyone standing close. No one was standing close.

He stopped moving. He stood with the scanner in his hand and waited.

She arrived faster than the first time. Less building, less the sense of a projection establishing itself and clarifying -- she was simply there, between him and the eastern wall, the white of her hair very bright in the lantern light. Clearer than the mine glimpse from weeks ago, not as clear as the quarters projection. Somewhere between, and more urgent than either.

She did not wait for him to speak. She did not begin with his name.

"Drayton's people are in the region," she said.

Not the tone of someone alarmed -- the tone of someone who had been tracking something for a long time and had reached the point where the tracking produced an action item. She spoke the way she had spoken before: with the certainty of someone who already knew how the conversation ended.

"Not at this colony. But within three days' travel. They have instruments that read Stellarium signatures. The signal from your shard has been ambiguous to them -- the plateau's mineral density produces interference. That interference will not last indefinitely." She paused the way she paused: not uncertainty, but the weight of what came next. "The Valley is not safe forever. Neither is Desolation's Edge."

He said: "What does Drayton want?"

"The same thing you want," she said. "Access to what Stellarium can do. He is Varr-descended and does not know it. He has been following the signal for years without understanding what he is following. He will not hesitate when he arrives."

The shard in his pocket was throwing light he could see against the shaft wall. He was aware of this and could not do anything about it.

"You have been looking for eleven years." Her voice had changed register -- not softer, but more direct, the directness of someone who had been patient for as long as patience served and had run the calculation on a different variable. "Sanctuary Valley has what you're looking for. The Stellarium you want, and more -- the full history of it, what it is, where it comes from, what it can do. Everything you cannot find here. Everything you have been trying to find." She let that sit. "Come."

She faded. Not the gradual settling of the first projection -- faster, more efficient, the way you ended a conversation when the information had been delivered and more time was someone else's to spend.

The shard in his pocket dimmed to its new level: not the flare of projection, not the nothing it had been before, but a steady visible glow that was going to be difficult to explain to anyone who noticed it.

He stood in the shaft for a moment with the scanner still in his hand.

The eastern wall had the same banded silicates it had always had. The bore smelled the same. The lantern light threw the same shadows. Nalani had been here and was not here, and Drayton's people were three days out, and the shard in his pocket was no longer something he could put back in the tin and file.

He went back to the survey. He had two hours left in the block and the data was not going to log itself. He worked through them with the shard against his hip and the name *Drayton* sitting in the back of his mind, not examined, just present, the way a new geological feature was present when you first clocked it and hadn't yet decided what it meant.

## Scene 4 -- The Decision

He finished the shift.

He ate at Okafor's with the specific appetite of someone who has been underground for eight hours and needs fuel rather than food. He did not talk to anyone. He noted, peripherally, that Ferris was at his usual table, eating with the same efficiency he brought to everything else, and that the sight of this produced nothing in particular, which was the right-sized response, and he moved past it.

He went back to his quarters.

He sat at the desk with the shard on the surface in front of him -- he'd taken it out of his pocket when he came in, placed it on the desk rather than returning it to the tin, because returning it to the tin felt like a statement he wasn't ready to make. It sat there glowing with its cold light in the dimness of his quarters and he looked at it and thought about the decision the way he thought about difficult geological assessments: not emotionally, not narratively, but practically. Building from available data toward the option that made the most sense under the conditions that existed.

*Condition: he had been looking for Stellarium for eleven years.*

Not passively. Actively. The shard, the northeastern surveys he kept returning to, the reading he'd done in year two on Stellarium mineralogy and in year four on the Varr civilization and in year seven on Dissenter community records, which were hard to find and largely incomplete, but the incompleteness was itself a data point. He had been building a case. Building it the way you built a geological case: accumulating evidence without announcing the hypothesis, because announcing the hypothesis before the evidence supported it was how you poisoned the record.

*Condition: he had a specific location.*

Not a rumor. Not a regional anomaly his scanner had flagged twice in the same quadrant. A name, confirmed by someone who had been there. Someone who had found him specifically -- not a person matching his description, his words back at him -- and delivered information she could not have had by any conventional means. The tin box contents. The note's exact text. The shard's name in a language that predated this sector's recorded history.

*Condition: the timeline was not open.*

Three days. Instruments calibrated for Stellarium signatures. A signal that had been ambiguous and would not stay ambiguous.

*Condition: the dream.*

He had not intended to list this as a condition. He listed it anyway, because leaving it off would mean treating the data selectively, and treating data selectively was how you produced records that looked useful and were wrong. The dream had given him a face and the nothing had taken it, and what remained was the specific shape of something absent. That shape was data. He did not know what it was data about. But it was a condition, the way the shard's behavior was a condition -- something external to him that had produced a change in the operational environment.

*Condition: the work he had been doing here was not the work.*

He sat with that for a moment. He had been a miner for eleven years and he was a good miner and the mining had given him structure and income and the specific satisfaction of competence, and none of that was nothing. But it was not what he had come here to find. He had come here to find Stellarium. He had found eleven years of wages and a tin box with a glowing shard in it and a set of survey logs that were excellent and changed nothing.

He got out the journal.

He opened it to the current page. The last entry: *Northeast survey complete. Anomalies resolved. Clean shift.* He wrote under it:

*I'm going to Sanctuary Valley.*

He closed the journal and put it back in the drawer.

His mind had already moved past the decision to the logistics. Not the commitment -- the commitment was done, it was in the journal, it was a completed action -- but the crossing. What he knew about the terrain. What she had said about travel distance. What that implied about route, conditions, time, what he'd need to carry, what he could source at DE before he left. Whether he filed anything with the claim board or simply stopped showing up. People stopped showing up at DE without announcement. That was the standard procedure.

He needed to find the route. She had said within travel distance and had not given coordinates, which was something he needed to solve before he could plan anything else. He needed to talk to Sill about the scanner -- not for repair, for calibration to a different kind of survey, a setting Sill would recognize and not ask about, because Sill had been here fifteen years and understood that some searches required different instruments than the company's standard kit.

He added these to the list he was building in working memory, arranging automatically into priority order, because that was how his mind handled logistics.

He had work in the morning. It was not the survey.

He had a different kind of work now, and he understood the difference, and the difference was the most solid thing he had felt in eleven years.
