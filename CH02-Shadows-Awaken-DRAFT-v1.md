# Chapter 2 — Mystery and Theme

## Scene 1 — The Shard Responds

The morning began the way all mornings began at Desolation's Edge: the alarm at 0500, the overhead light coming on at a level that didn't ask permission, the specific weight of recycled air that told him before he was fully awake that nothing had changed while he slept.

He sat up. Swung his feet to the floor. Stood.

The tin box was on the shelf.

He had a system for mornings, and the system worked by not thinking. Wash up, dress, eat something at the canteen, check his equipment at the locker station, drop into the mine. The system held eleven years of mornings together the way the support struts held the mine shafts: not complicated, reliable, not worth examining too closely. He moved through it the way he moved through everything: efficiently, without waste.

He was three steps from the tin box when it hummed.

Not like last night. Last night had been brief, faint, the kind of thing his sleep-deprived brain might have produced without assistance. This was cleaner. Sustained. The hum had a quality to it that the one last night hadn't: direction. Like a compass needle that had been drifting for years finally settling toward north.

He stopped.

Stood still for a moment. The ambient sounds of the colony filled in around the silence: the recycling units cycling, a distant conveyor running its first pass of the morning, the low mechanical register of the mine head two kilometers north. Nothing unusual. Nothing that would produce the kind of resonance he'd just heard.

He crossed to the box and opened it.

The shard sat where it always sat, behind the locket and the folded note. He picked it up. Held it flat on his palm the way he always did, waiting for the response he usually got: nothing. The shard sitting inert and cool, gray as river stone in the flat overhead light.

The hum was real.

Low frequency, barely a vibration. He pressed his thumb along the longest edge and felt it more than heard it, a faint oscillation that had no business coming from a piece of mineral he'd been carrying for eleven years without incident. He tilted his hand. The sensation didn't follow the tilt the way something caused by the shard's own mass would. It was pointed at something. Something outside the room, outside the colony, somewhere in a direction he didn't have a name for.

He stood there for a full minute.

Then he ran through the possibilities the way he ran through every problem: methodically, building toward an explanation that satisfied. Vibration from the morning conveyor startup — he'd felt that frequency in the walls, not in his hand. A new vein being worked in the adjacent shaft, the resonance traveling through the rock — possible, worth checking, except the sensation was directional in a way that geological resonance wasn't. Equipment interference from his scanner, still on the work bench — he checked. The scanner was off.

No explanation found. He filed the absence of one.

He put the shard back. Closed the tin. Standing in his quarters analyzing a piece of mineral that had been inexplicable for eleven years was not going to make it explicable in the next ten minutes. He'd spent his first three years on DE trying. He'd learned to file it and move.

He went to work.

## Scene 2 — The Theme Spoken

He was running a lateral extension off the eastern survey corridor when Petersen made his comment.

Petersen was one of the fixtures of the Heliosanthus Mines: a man in his mid-fifties who had been on Desolation's Edge long enough that the mine had shaped itself around his presence rather than the other way around. He had the specific quality of someone who had arrived young, decided to stay, and stopped dividing the world into past and future somewhere around the ten-year mark. Not unhappy. Not broken by the place. Settled in a way that was complete enough to look like peace and, in certain lights, troubling enough to look like something else.

They'd worked adjacent sections dozens of times across the years. They talked about the geology when they needed to and not otherwise. Jax preferred it that way. Petersen had learned to.

They'd been at it for two hours, running parallel survey passes through a formation the automated systems had flagged as potentially stratified. The work was methodical and required enough attention to keep the mind from wandering. Jax was logging a stress concentration in the upper face when Petersen spoke.

"Nobody finds Stellarium," Petersen said. Not to Jax specifically. Somewhere between himself and the rock. "It finds them."

Jax marked the concentration and moved his scanner eighteen centimeters.

"That's religion talking," he said. "Stellarium is a mineral. It's in the ground somewhere and I'm going to find it."

Petersen shrugged. The shrug covered everything: agreement, disagreement, the fundamental indifference of a man who had made his peace with what the ground did and didn't give up. He went back to his pass. Jax went back to his.

The exchange lasted eight seconds. Neither of them thought much of it afterward.

He'd had the same argument with himself in his first year on DE, when the obsession was new enough to feel embarrassing. Stellarium drawn to a person, a person drawn to Stellarium, some cosmic arrangement that put the right mineral in the hands of the right finder. He'd dismissed it in year one for the same reason he dismissed it now: it was comfortable thinking. It made the search feel inevitable rather than effortful. It let a person stop looking because they trusted the finding would come on its own schedule.

Jax did not stop looking. He had not stopped for eleven years. Whatever Stellarium did or didn't do, it wasn't going to find him sitting still.

He moved the scanner and kept working.

## Scene 3 — The Dream Returns

The dream came back at 0230.

Not the standard version. Not just the flash and the pain and the nothing. He'd been through enough iterations to know the standard version by its shape, the way it arrived and ran its course and released him back into the dark with his heart doing the thing it did and the ceiling above him unchanged. The standard version had structure. He'd built a relationship with it across eleven years the way you built a relationship with a recurring injury: learned its onset, learned its duration, learned not to expect it to be better this time.

This was different.

The flash came the way it always came. The pain came the way it always came. And then, in the space before the nothing, in the fractional moment between sensation and absence that he'd learned to recognize as the threshold, there was something that hadn't been there before.

A room.

Not clearly. Not held. Walls, maybe, and a quality of light that was interior rather than open, and the suggestion of a surface at chest height that might have been a table or a counter or something else entirely. It was there for less than a second. It was gone before any of his processing could reach it. And then the nothing came and the nothing was the same as it always was, and he woke with his heart doing something inconvenient.

He lay still.

The ceiling of his quarters was pale concrete, cracked at the diagonal he'd tracked across eleven years. The air was the air it always was. He lay still and let his heart work through whatever it needed to work through, because he had learned that arguing with the heartbeat didn't help and moving too fast made the floor feel wrong.

He did not open the tin box. He thought about it. The thought was brief and he set it aside with the same motion he used for everything he wasn't going to act on: noted, filed, not pursued.

He stared at the ceiling.

A room. Not a face, not a voice, not anything that carried narrative weight. Just walls, light, a surface. Something that might have been familiar and might have been invention and was gone too fast to distinguish between the two. He'd had eleven years of the dream and this was the first time it had shown him anything other than the flash and the nothing.

He did not know whether to call that progress.

After a while, ten minutes, maybe twelve, his heart returned to something he recognized as normal. He got up, because lying still had run its course and getting up was what came next.

He filled a glass of water, drank it at the window, watched the mine head's running lights in the dark outside. The lights didn't go out. They never went out; the mine ran on a twenty-three-hour cycle and this hour was part of it. The most reliable thing on Desolation's Edge. He'd been watching them since his first week here and they hadn't changed.

He put the glass down. He went back to bed.

He did not sleep well. In the morning he wrote in his journal: *The dream again. Something new. Don't know what to make of it yet.* He looked at the entry for a moment, then closed the journal and went to work.
