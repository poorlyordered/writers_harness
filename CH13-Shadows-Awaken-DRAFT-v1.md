# Chapter 13

## Scene 1 — The Opening

The silence between them had been comfortable for about twenty minutes. It was beginning not to be.

The rest of the team was occupied in the ways the rest of the team occupied themselves: Toby had found something wrong with the stabilizer coupling that required his complete attention and Quill's partial attention and a significant portion of the maintenance bay floor. Mira had taken a position at the forward observation panel and was watching whatever Mira watched when she was watching nothing in particular. Aria-7 was reviewing navigational data and occasionally posing clarifying questions into the middle distance.

Which left Jax and Lena in the ship's small common space, each with work in front of them, and a silence that had become the kind that required deliberate maintenance to sustain.

She looked up first.

"The shard," she said. "When it responds to you — is there a direction to it, or is it more like a general warmth?"

He looked at her. "Both. Depends on whether I'm moving toward something or standing still."

"And after the raid on DE — it ran cooler than usual after the flare."

She hadn't been at DE for the raid. She was piecing it together from what she'd read or been told. He said, "Pulled heat from me to recover. First time it did that."

She made the sound of someone filing information that connects to something she already has. A small cylindrical device had appeared in her left hand, and she was turning it between her fingers in the way of someone who didn't know they were doing it.

"It's drawing from your body heat to recharge," she said. "That suggests a thermal interface — the shard isn't self-powering. It borrows."

"And returns it."

She looked at him with a faint adjustment — the recalibration of someone who had expected a different kind of follow-up and received a better one. "Yes. The warmth you feel when it's oriented — it's giving back what it took. The interface is reciprocal." A pause. "Which means it isn't just a power source. It's a relationship."

"Between the shard and the person carrying it."

"Between the shard and a *specific* person." She said this with care. "Stellarium doesn't do this generally. The interface only activates with certain bloodlines. For everyone else it's inert. What you're describing — the direction, the warmth, the thermal draw — none of that happens without the right carrier."

"And the wrong carrier gets the fever."

"The fever is what happens when the interface activates but there's no framework to contextualize it." She chose *framework* deliberately. He noticed the choosing and filed it.

"Nalani calls it guidance," he said.

"Yes." Something in her face settled. "That's accurate."

He thought about the shard cooling in his hand that morning on DE. Pulling warmth to recover. The relationship language fit in the way that useful descriptions fit — not explaining more than it needed to. "So the shard has been borrowing from me for eleven years."

"And returning it. Every time you felt it orient, every pulse — that's the exchange completing." She paused, then said, as if she hadn't quite planned to: "It's been calibrating to you the whole time. Learning your specific thermal signature. That's why it responds to you differently than it would to anyone else, even someone with the same bloodline."

He looked at her. "You know a lot about this for someone who just joined the mission."

The recalibration was visible this time — a slight shift in how she held herself, not defensive, just registering that she'd said more than she'd intended. "I've been researching Stellarium for years," she said. "Theoretically. The interface mechanics were— I've had reason to understand them."

She had chosen that phrasing carefully. He noticed the care without knowing what it was covering.

"What drew you to it," he said. "Stellarium specifically."

She turned the device between her fingers. "An experiment that went wrong," she said. "I needed to understand what I'd been working with." She said this with the flatness of someone delivering accurate information and nothing more.

He accepted it at the same flatness. They sat with it.

## Scene 1.5 — The Intrusion

Toby appeared in the common space doorway with the energy of a man who had solved one problem and discovered it contained a second problem.

"Jax. The fuel calculation for the third leg — Quill thinks we're six percent light on the estimate. Do you want to look at it now or should we adjust at the next stop?"

"Next stop," Jax said. "There's margin."

"That's what I told him." Toby looked between them with the quick social intelligence of someone who read rooms as a professional habit. He didn't remark on what he read. "Right. Continuing." He looked at Lena. "You settling in all right?"

"Yes," she said. "Thank you."

"Good. This ship is better than it looks," he said, with the tone of someone who had said this before and still mostly believed it. He disappeared back toward the maintenance bay.

The silence that returned after he left was different from the silence before. It had been witnessed now — a third person had passed through it and kept moving, which meant it was real enough to be noticed and not so uncomfortable that it required comment. They were both aware of this.

She looked back at her device. He looked back at the map. They let it settle.

"Go on," he said.

She looked up. "Sorry?"

"You were about to say something. Before he came in."

She considered this. Then: "I was going to ask why Stellarium specifically. For you."

## Scene 2 — The Real Question

They sat with that for a moment. From the maintenance bay Toby described the second coupling problem to Quill with the enthusiasm of someone who found mechanical failure aesthetically interesting.

"You've been looking for Stellarium for eleven years," she said. The tone of someone for whom curiosity was both professional mode and genuine disposition.

"Since I woke up on DE."

"With the shard already on you."

"In the tin box. Yes."

The device turned between her fingers. "Most people who encounter Stellarium — even those with the fever — don't sustain it for eleven years. They burn out, or redirect, or—" She stopped. Restarted. "What kept you."

"The mission."

"But you didn't know it was a mission. You thought it was prospecting."

"I thought I was following a lead."

"A lead that came in a tin box with no name attached."

"Yes."

"For eleven years."

"Yes."

She was quiet. The device turned slowly. Outside, Toby had escalated his assessment of the coupling to something approaching genuine structural concern.

"Why?" she said.

Just that. Not *what are you looking for* — he'd answered that version already. Not *what do you expect to find*. The question behind those questions: why does it matter so much.

It landed differently than the others had. On DE, nobody asked that. The answer was self-evident to anyone living there: Stellarium was the job, the conviction, the thing you didn't examine because examination was beside the point of doing it. She was asking as if it wasn't self-evident. As if there was an answer that went somewhere past mission and obsession and eleven years of waking up in the same bunk with the same signal in the same pocket.

He looked at his hands for a moment. Scarred hands. The kind that came from years of the same work in the same rock. Eleven years of a certainty he couldn't explain and had never tried to, because trying to would have meant admitting it needed explaining.

"Because it's the only thing that's ever felt like mine," he said.

He said it the way he said things that were simply true — flat, without ceremony, the way you state a fact about load-bearing rock. He had held this belief for eleven years. It didn't require defense. It barely required saying. He said it because she had asked and because evasion would have cost him something he wasn't willing to spend.

She went very still.

Not the stillness of processing. A different kind — the kind that happens when something arrives that is too large for the available space and the person experiencing it must hold very carefully until they find somewhere to put it down. Her hands stopped. The device was motionless between her fingers. She was looking at him with the monitoring quality in her eyes locked down harder than usual, and behind it something he could feel the weight of without being able to name.

He watched her do this without knowing what he was watching.

After a moment she said, "That makes sense." Her voice was level. The kind of level that was a production rather than a natural state.

"Does it," he said.

"The shard was there when you woke up. The conviction was there. For someone with no history—" She was translating it into cause and effect, into the framework she used for everything. He could hear the translation happening. He could also hear the effort it required. "The one thing that predates the nothing would feel like the most fundamental fact about yourself."

He looked at her. She was looking at something near his collar.

Then she looked away.

The locket. The same look from the meeting room — that contained quality, the brief redirection. He still didn't have a category for it and the absence of a category was beginning to feel like a category of its own.

"You're good at translating things," he said.

Something moved across her face that wasn't quite a smile. "Occupational habit."

"What's the untranslated version."

She was quiet for a moment — the monitoring, the careful calibration. Then: "That it must have been very hard to not know what was yours."

He didn't have anything to say to that. It was the most direct thing she'd said in the conversation and it had arrived without warning and the only thing he had to offer was that she was right and that he had no idea how she knew that with the specific precision she'd used to say it.

"It was," he said.

## Scene 3 — After

They found their way to something lighter after that, the way conversations sometimes do after touching something real — not by avoiding what happened but by moving past it into the ordinary frequency of two people who have established that they can talk to each other.

She asked about the transit route. She'd been reviewing Toby's navigation and had a question about the third system leg. He answered what he knew. She noted something about Stellarium concentrations along that corridor that might be relevant to calibration work before they reached the Valley. He asked what kind of calibration. She explained. The explanation was technical and he followed most of it, and the part he didn't follow he asked about, and she answered with the same precise patience she brought to everything except the moments when precision required effort.

It was easier than it should have been. He noticed that without examining it.

He also noticed that she never fully relaxed. The device was back in motion, turning slowly. She was present, engaged, genuinely here in the conversation — and underneath it, something was running parallel that she was keeping on a separate track.

He had spent eleven years reading terrain for what it wasn't telling him. He knew the shape of managed information.

When Aria-7 called from the forward section — a navigational anomaly, Lena's assessment required — she gathered her kit efficiently, the way she did everything.

"Thank you," she said. She meant more than the words covered.

"For what," he said.

She looked at him for a moment. "The honest answer."

She left. He heard her and Aria-7 begin a technical exchange in the forward section — Aria-7's calibrated voice, Lena's quieter one, the rhythm of two people who thought in similar registers and arrived there differently.

He sat with the conversation.

*The only thing that's ever felt like mine.* He'd said it and meant it and it was true and there was nothing further to examine. The shard was his. The conviction was his. The eleven years of following the signal — his. He had chosen each leg, every morning on DE, every shift in the same rock. Nobody had made him stay. Nobody had kept him searching.

He almost believed this completely.

The stillness after that line. He kept returning to it. Not the stillness of someone who hadn't understood — she'd understood it immediately, had translated it before the echo faded. It was the stillness of someone who had understood something additional that he hadn't said. Something the sentence carried that he wasn't aware it was carrying.

The locket. She'd looked at it twice now — once in the meeting room, once midway through the conversation, both times with that contained quality, that immediate redirection. He had worn it for eleven years. He had no idea whose face was inside it. He had never opened it far enough to find out because opening it far enough felt like something he wasn't ready to do, though he had no name for the not-readiness.

She had seen the locket and something had happened in her face before she controlled it.

*It must have been very hard to not know what was yours.*

She had said that with the precision of someone who knew something specific. Not the broad empathy a stranger might offer — not *that sounds difficult* or *I can imagine.* The precision of someone working from actual information toward a conclusion they were not going to state.

He turned it over once and put it down. Not enough to conclude anything useful. The column of things he was filing without explanation was growing, and he had learned on DE that columns like this eventually either resolved or became the problem. This one didn't feel like it was going to resolve quietly.

He pulled out the route map and got back to work.

Tomorrow Nalani started the training. He had more pressing things to understand than a conversation he couldn't fully account for.

The locket was where it always was.
