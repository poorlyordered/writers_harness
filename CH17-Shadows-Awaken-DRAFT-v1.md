# Chapter 17

## Scene 1 — The Contact

Mira had been at the forward observation panel for eleven minutes without moving.

Not unusual in itself — she spent significant time there doing the kind of looking that wasn't quite looking at anything specific. But Jax had been watching her for the last four of those eleven minutes, because the quality of her stillness had changed two minutes ago in a way he couldn't name but recognized. It was the stillness of someone who had identified something and was running through its implications before saying anything.

He moved to stand beside her. He looked at what she was looking at.

"Three contacts," she said. "Running dark. They've been paralleling our heading for thirty-eight minutes."

Transit space outside appeared clear — it always appeared clear when someone running dark didn't want to be seen. "How are you reading them."

"Displacement. They're moving through debris scatter on the outbound lane. The pattern is there if you've been watching long enough." She had been. "I wanted a complete picture before I said anything."

Aria-7 was already at her station, pulling the sensor data Mira's notation had flagged. "Confirmed. Three vessels, running with Stellarium-adjacent dampening on their emission signatures. The dampening is not standard — it is more sophisticated than anything in registered fleet inventories." She paused in the specific way she paused when the pause was required. "Their convergence point with this ship's current position: approximately eighteen minutes."

Quill looked up from the sensor cluster. "Eighteen. Or now, if that estimate's already old."

"Sixteen," Aria-7 said. "Approximately."

Toby, from the cockpit: "I'm going to start looking at what this space offers, because I am not willing to just wait for the convergence."

"Do that," Jax said.

He read the panel the way he read geological surveys — looking for what the data implied about what lay beneath. Three ships with sophisticated Stellarium-adjacent dampening and a twenty-year operation behind them were not a probe. A probe was two or three operatives on a dock. This was the operation itself.

He pressed his palm against his sternum. The shard was running hot.

"Toby. Distance to the next waypoint."

"Forty-one minutes at current speed."

Twenty-three minutes of open space before they reached anything. He looked at the panel and thought: *this is what Drayton built while I was in the rock.*

## Scene 2 — The Gap

The sixteen minutes became twelve before the first ship broke its running-dark configuration and appeared on active sensors. A deliberate choice — not a failure of their concealment. They had been seen and had decided to let themselves be seen. The transition from covert to overt was the signal: *we are no longer interested in whether you know we're here.*

Toby had found something useful: a debris field from an old extraction operation, dense enough to make targeting unreliable and thin enough to navigate. He was taking them into it without being asked, which was the most useful thing anyone could have done.

The first contact came through the debris field at the ship's stern — a Stellarium-adjacent energy pulse that hit the rear shielding and made the hull ring like struck metal. Aria-7, immediately: "That frequency. Filing it. The signature has a pattern I have seen before but not in this context. Continue."

Jax moved to the port airlock — the angle that gave him the best direct line to two of the three ships if he could project through the gap in the debris field. His working theory from Ch 16: use the geometry, aim at a structure, let the deflection do the work. There were structures in the debris field. He had eleven years of knowing what happened when force met rock of various densities.

He set the intention. The shard came up.

He released it. The debris field deflection worked — the output hit a dense ore fragment and scattered across the nearest ship's approach vector, disrupting the second energy pulse before it reached them. Precision: approximately correct. Scale: sufficient for one.

The second ship adjusted and fired. The third held position. Two simultaneous threats at different angles, and the deflection point he'd used was spent — the debris had dispersed. He reached again and felt what the situation was asking for: two outputs, coordinated, for different deflection geometries, with the precision to miss the debris he didn't want to hit.

He couldn't hold both. He could shape one or he could release both and shape neither.

He released both. Neither was shaped correctly.

The second ship's pulse hit the hull — not the shielding, the hull itself. A different sound. Lower. More structural. Toby's voice, level: "Hull breach in cargo hold. Minor. Sealing." Then: "I'm going to do something with the debris field. Brace."

He did something with the debris field. Jax didn't see what — he was tracking the third ship's approach vector — but the tactical geometry shifted. The third ship broke its angle to avoid whatever Toby had created, and that gave Mira her window.

The boarding breach came through the starboard maintenance access — a two-person team that had been waiting for the hull impact's distraction. Mira was there. The detail of what happened next was brief and sufficient. When Jax reached the corridor thirty seconds later, she was sealing the access and there was no one to deal with.

Quill, from the engineering bay: "I've found their coordination channel. Three ships, Stellarium-adjacent synchronization protocol — they're sharing targeting data in real time. If I interrupt it the coordination breaks." He said this with the focused interest of someone who had found a new problem rather than the alarm it probably warranted. "I need four minutes."

"Do it," Jax said.

"I'll provide cover analysis," Aria-7 said. "Quill, sending you the frequency modulation pattern now."

Four minutes in an active engagement was not four minutes in ordinary time. Jax held the corridor. Toby held the ship in the debris field. Mira held the starboard access. Aria-7 talked Quill through something technical that Jax did not follow and did not need to follow.

Four minutes.

"Done," Quill said. The third ship's approach vector stuttered — a visible hesitation, a loss of the coordination that had been making the three-ship operation coherent. The first ship adjusted to compensate. The second was still dealing with the debris field. The window was brief.

Jax used it. One output, one target, full intention set beforehand. The emotional register behind it was different from anything in the trials — not *forward*, not the eleven-year conviction, but something more bounded and specific: *enough.* It went through the first ship's Stellarium-adjacent emitter with a precision that was the closest he'd come to the ability's designed form. Not destroyed. Disabled.

The first ship broke off. The second followed. The third held position for a moment longer — long enough to feel deliberate — then left at a speed that said *this is a choice* and not *this is defeat.*

## Scene 3 — The Withdrawal and What Aria-7 Noticed

They held the debris field position for twenty minutes after the third ship's departure. Mira's call. The right one — long enough to confirm the withdrawal was real and not a repositioning. Then Toby brought them out and back onto the route heading, and nobody said anything consequential for a while.

The cargo hold breach was superficial. Toby's assessment, then Quill's, then Aria-7's confirmation. Structural integrity held. The maintenance access Mira had sealed was sealed. The Stellarium-adjacent emitter Jax had disabled: that ship was running blind on its coordination frequency, which bought time before Drayton's operation could reconfigure.

Time. Not safety. Both of those were different things and they both knew it.

Aria-7 found him after Quill and Toby had moved to the repair assessment and Mira had returned to her forward position. He was sitting in the corridor — he'd been holding it and hadn't moved, and sitting had seemed like the next logical thing — and she stood at the edge of his field of view.

"I noticed something in the engagement," she said. "In the Stellarium-adjacent activity. Not in their tactics — in the energy signatures themselves."

"Tell me."

"When you were actively using the interface — particularly during the second release, when you sent both outputs without shaping them — the Stellarium-adjacent frequency in their coordination protocol responded. Not to the outputs themselves. To the interface being active." She said this with the precision she always used and with a quality he had not heard from her before: the quality of someone who had located the edge of their own certainty and was speaking from it. "The response pattern has an architectural character. It resembles something in the Varr records I have access to, but I have not yet located the specific reference. It is consistent with a designed interaction — not an accident."

He looked at her. "The ability interacted with their technology."

"The interface interacted with their Stellarium-adjacent systems in a way that neither the outputs nor the systems' standard behavior would predict. The interaction has a pattern." A pause. "I wanted you to know I had noticed it."

"Is it a vulnerability — in them or in us."

"I don't know yet." She said this without apparent difficulty at the admission. "Both are possible. The pattern needs to be understood before I can determine the direction."

"How long."

"I don't know that either." She met his eyes with the directness she used when she had delivered everything she had. "When I know, I'll tell you."

"All right," he said.

She returned to her station.

He sat in the corridor longer. The shard was warming against his sternum in its slow way. The ache in his chest was receding in the same patient increments it always receded, the thermal exchange running its course.

Three ships. Coordinated, sophisticated, running technology that Aria-7 had no prior record of. A boarding team that had timed itself to the hull impact. A withdrawal that had been a deliberate choice by someone who had assessed the engagement and determined the math didn't favor them continuing.

He had held the corridor. Toby had held the debris field. Mira had held the starboard access. Quill had broken their coordination channel in four minutes. He had disabled one emitter with one precise output in the right register with the right intention set before he opened the channel.

He had not done this alone.

He didn't say that. He sat with it in the corridor, and it was true, and he was going to have to learn to hold it as a fact rather than as a variable in a calculation he was still running.

Mira appeared at the far end of the corridor. She looked at him — the patient, measuring look — and then she went back to her position. She hadn't said anything. She didn't need to.
