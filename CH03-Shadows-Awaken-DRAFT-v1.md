# Chapter 3 -- Link to Antagonist

## Scene 1 -- News Moves Through the Colony

The news came through Okafor's the way most things came through Okafor's: without announcement, carried in pieces by three different people who each had a fraction of it, assembled by whoever was paying attention when the pieces arrived.

Jax was on his second cup of canteen-grade coffee when the first piece came in: a convoy worker off an overnight corridor run, talking to the table behind him about a mining crew that hadn't reported. Three sectors over. Standard operation, no outstanding safety flags, two comms cycles of nothing.

He didn't turn around. He listened.

The second piece came from the equipment bay, two hours later, from a man at the adjacent locker bank relaying something he'd heard from the night shift foreman. The missing crew's last comms log had been reviewed. Nothing unusual in it: a scan result, a minor equipment flag, a note about weather visibility. Then nothing. Equipment pinged at six-hour intervals and then stopped pinging.

The man said the word "fast" twice. He said it the way people said a word when they were trying to contain the implication of it.

Jax checked his scanner charge and said nothing.

The third piece came from the shift assignment queue. Two workers had transferred in from a smaller extraction operation -- independent, positioned between DE and the missing crew's sector. They were quiet about what they'd seen. The kind of quiet that was the product of having processed a shock and emerged with a single fact they'd decided to carry without examining. One of them said, when pressed by someone in line, that it had not looked like an accident. That whoever had done it knew what they were doing and had done it before.

What it looked like beyond that she didn't say. Nobody pressed her for it.

Jax took his assignment and went to work.

He ran through it the way he ran through every problem: not rushing toward a conclusion, building the picture from what was actually in it. A crew gone silent. Equipment that stopped pinging. A competitor's operation shut down in a way that carried the signature of intention. Workers arriving with a shock they were learning to carry.

Someone was moving through the region. Moving with purpose, moving in a way that knew what it was doing. The shape of it was legible. The reason underneath it -- what someone would want badly enough to work the region this way -- he didn't have yet.

He filed the absence of an answer and went into the mine.

## Scene 2 -- The Shard Pulls

His assignment took him into a sector he'd run twice before: a lateral extension off the eastern survey corridor, close enough to the missing crew's last-known position that on a quiet day he could have flagged their operation on his scanner's regional display.

He checked. The flag wasn't there.

He put the scanner to work.

He'd put the shard in his pocket that morning without examining why. It had stayed in his hand longer than it usually did when he was packing his kit, and when he'd closed the tin he'd slipped it into his pocket rather than back inside. He'd noted this and let it stand.

The shift was methodical. Two stress concentrations in the upper face, an interesting lateral stratification worth flagging for follow-up. The work required enough attention to keep the mind from wandering. He let it require that.

Midway through a pass along the eastern wall, the shard pulsed.

Not a hum. Not the sustained low frequency from the past two mornings. This was shorter and more forceful -- a single beat through his palm and up his wrist before he'd registered what it was. He went still.

The shard pulsed again.

He took it out of his pocket and held it flat on his palm. The shaft around him was unchanged: pale fractured geology, the distant conveyors, the regular mechanical register of an operation running the way it was supposed to run. His scanner registered nothing anomalous.

A third pulse. Oriented -- he felt it the same way he'd felt the direction in his quarters, pointed at something specific rather than drifting. He turned slowly, keeping his hand level. The pulse strengthened as he faced northeast. Toward the sector where the crew had last reported.

He stood there for a moment.

Then he picked up his tools and went northeast.

## Scene 3 -- What the Shard Found

He told himself he was checking the boundary. Verifying that the adjacent sector was what his regional map said it was, confirming there was no geological overlap with his own survey area. This was not entirely a lie. A miner who understood the formations in neighboring sectors did better work in his own.

He went northeast for ten minutes before he stopped telling himself anything.

The shard had gone from pulsing to something more continuous -- not quite the sustained hum from his quarters, but a pressure that came in fast, close intervals, rising as he moved. He kept his hand level and followed it the way he'd learned to follow a signal: not chasing, not forcing, just keeping the direction and letting the distance close.

The geology changed as he moved. The pale fractured rock of his own sector gave way to something older and less worked: darker stone, stress fractures running in a different pattern, surfaces that hadn't seen scanner light recently. At the edge of his lantern's reach he could make out survey markers from another operation -- not current, not recent. He kept moving.

The shard pulsed once more, strong enough that he felt it in his teeth.

Then it went quiet.

He stopped.

She was standing at the far edge of the lantern light. Or not standing -- present there, at the far edge of what the light could reach, in the way that something becomes visible at a threshold before you've understood what you're looking at. Small. White hair, loose around her shoulders. He had the impression of age without the appearance of frailty -- the opposite of that, actually, something deliberate and settled in the posture, like a person who had arrived early and was simply waiting for the rest of the room to catch up.

She was looking at him.

Not past him, not at the space around him. At him. Directly. With the specific quality of someone who had been looking for a very long time and had now found what they were looking for, and had no particular urgency about it because they had always known this moment would come.

She didn't speak. She didn't move.

She was translucent in the way that nothing solid was ever translucent -- he could see the fractured rock behind her, the survey markers, the geometry of the shaft continuing into darkness. But she was not a trick of the light. She was not a shadow his tired brain had assembled from the shapes around him. She was looking at him and he was looking at her and something in his chest registered this as real in the way that very few things registered as real before the reasoning mind could argue them away.

He took a step back.

She remained. Still looking. Still unhurried.

He took another step back, turned, and walked the way he'd come at a pace that was not quite a run because a run required acknowledging what he was running from.

He returned to his survey area. He picked up his scanner. He logged the remaining concentrations, completed his passes, and brought his sheet out of the mine when the shift-end signal ran: three long pulses, one short.

He did not write it in his kit notes. He did not file it under anything. He put the shard back in his pocket on the walk out and kept his eyes forward and his hands busy with the tools he was returning to the equipment bay.

## Scene 4 -- The Look He Recognized

Heliosanthus was low on the horizon when he came out. The dust in the air caught the light and turned everything amber: the mine head structures, the ore-processing towers, the long column of end-of-shift workers walking the corridor back toward the residential blocks. He joined them and let the rhythm carry him.

He was three blocks from the residential section when he passed the Keeper Quarter.

At this hour the cluster of older buildings had its usual quality: the stillness that persisted even when the rest of DE was in motion, the specific way the buildings sat relative to each other that looked like it had been thought through. He had passed this cluster enough times to have stopped noticing it and started noticing it again, in the way that recurring things occasionally surface from habit into sight.

An older woman was outside, working on the door frame of the building nearest the street. Repair work: a sealant tool in one hand, a length of gasket material on the step. She heard him -- or felt him, or simply knew he was there, the way certain people knew things before the evidence arrived -- and looked up.

She held the look for a moment. Not a greeting, not hostility. Something quieter and more deliberate than either, with an attentiveness underneath it that did not quite match the task she'd been doing.

Then she returned to her work.

He kept walking.

He was half a block past when it hit him. Not the look itself -- the quality of it. The directness. The unhurried weight of being seen by someone who did not need anything from you but had registered your presence as significant anyway. The sense of a patience that preceded you and would continue after you were gone.

He had been on the receiving end of that quality once already today.

He stopped walking.

A woman in amber lantern light at the edge of a dark shaft. A woman in the amber end-of-shift glow at the edge of the Keeper Quarter. Both looking at him the same way. Both without a word.

The workers behind him shifted around his stopped frame without complaint. He was in the current long enough for his pulse to do something he didn't ask it to do.

Then he moved again. His quarters. The tin box on the shelf. The work he had to do tomorrow.

He did not look back at the Quarter.

He did not need to.
