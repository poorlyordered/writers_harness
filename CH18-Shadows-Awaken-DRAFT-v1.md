# Chapter 18

## Scene 1 — The Almost

The ship's common space was empty when Lena came through, and then it wasn't.

She had the small diagnostic device in her hand — the one he'd seen her turn between her fingers during their conversation in the common space — and she was reviewing something on it with the quality of someone who had come to this space for quiet and found it already occupied. She paused in the doorway. A fraction of a second, not quite deciding.

She came in.

He was at the table with the route map spread flat — he could have reviewed it on his tablet but the physical scale of the thing helped him think about physical distances. She took a position across the space, near the observation port, with the device in her hand and the quality of someone occupying a room rather than sharing one.

They let this be the shape of it for a few minutes.

"When you think about the time before DE," she said — she was looking at the observation port, not at him — "do you think about it as absence, or as something that was there and then wasn't?"

He looked up. The question had arrived prepared. She had been carrying it for a while before finding the conditions to ask it. "Absence," he said. "Like a shaft that hasn't been surveyed. It's not nothing — it's space that hasn't been entered yet."

"Most Stellarium-adjacent cases I've studied — the ones with significant memory disruption — the subjects described it as loss. Something that was there and is gone." The research register. Underneath it, something else that wasn't research. "What you're describing is different."

"It doesn't feel like loss. It feels like starting from a particular point."

She was quiet. The device had stilled in her fingers. "The shard was there when you started from that point."

"Yes."

"And the locket."

"Yes."

She looked at the port. He looked at her, and she had the quality he had learned to associate with a person standing at the edge of a decision — the specific stillness of someone who has been approaching something from a long distance and is now in the proximity of it and finding the proximity different from what the distance suggested.

He said: "There's something specific you're asking about."

She looked at him. The monitoring quality in her eyes was running close to the surface of this conversation rather than on its usual separate track. "The accident I mentioned," she said. "The experiment. I've been trying to decide how much of what I know about it is relevant to the mission." She chose *mission* with the care of someone selecting a word to hold a weight it might not be designed for.

"If it's relevant to the mission—"

"It may not be just the mission." Quiet. Not a correction — an admission of scale, partial, careful. The shape of what was behind it without being what was behind it.

He waited. He had learned in eleven years of extraction work that the moment when the thing you're trying to reach is closest is also the moment when forcing it produces the worst outcome. He waited the way he waited in a shaft showing signs of yield.

Something closed in her. He could see it happen — the same process as in the common space conversation, the careful redistribution of weight, the calibration that required visible effort and produced a surface of level that was the product of the effort, not its absence. She looked at the device.

"It's relevant," she said. "I'll tell you more when I've sorted out what the most useful framing is." Level voice. The production was very well made.

He let it be. Pressing would not produce the crossing — it would produce a closing. "All right," he said.

She looked at the route map. "The Veth corridor — there's a Stellarium concentration I've been wanting to flag. Relevant to the calibration work when we arrive." Back to the research register. Clean re-entry.

They talked about the Veth corridor. He filed the almost in the same column as the locket reaction at the rendezvous — when she had first come through the other door and looked at his collar and then looked away — the hesitations in the common space conversation. The growing record of specific things she was managing around him that were about him, not about operational caution.

His read, provisional: she knew something from the accident that connected to his lost past. Something specific. She was a materials scientist who had worked Stellarium before joining this mission. Whatever went wrong had consequences she was still carrying. He had some of those consequences in his inside pocket, which was probably why she'd looked at the locket the way she had.

He was approximately right about the shape of it. He was wrong about what it was.

## Scene 2 — Drayton's Varr Connection

Nalani's communication came through late in the ship's cycle, the quarter-second lag that had become the texture of her voice in transit. She had been waiting for the debris field intercept's data before disclosing this, she said — she needed to know how Drayton's forces were operating before she could contextualize what she was about to say.

The team gathered. Nalani said: "Cassius Drayton carries Varr bloodline. Catastrophe variant."

The silence that followed was the kind produced by information that the room has been half-expecting and still isn't ready for.

Aria-7, almost immediately: "That explains the architectural resonance. The pattern I filed from the debris field engagement — it is consistent with an active but unguided Varr bloodline interface. Uncontrolled Stellarium fever in someone who has had it for approximately—" A pause. "Fifteen to twenty years, based on the signature's density."

"Since he started building the operation," Quill said. Flat, like a calculation arriving. "He's not hunting Stellarium. He's hunting whatever Stellarium promised him." He looked at the table rather than at anyone specific. "He just doesn't know what that is, because he doesn't have the framework and he doesn't have a guide."

Nobody contradicted this. It was accurate.

Nalani, through the lag: "The catastrophe bloodline variant produces the fever without the directionality. The signal is present; the map is absent. Drayton has been running toward something he can feel but cannot name for nearly twenty years. His operation, his resources, his reach — this is what that looks like from the outside. He is not a man with a plan for Stellarium. He is a man who has been driven toward it by something he has misidentified as ambition."

Jax had said nothing. He was looking at the route map — he had brought it to the table without planning to, the physical object as something to look at when information required stillness.

The dark mirror had been half-visible since the beginning: a man who wanted Stellarium, a man who was the answer to what Stellarium was looking for. Same signal. Different configuration. Different outcome.

"He's been running on the fever for twenty years without training," he said.

"Yes," Nalani said.

"What does that do to someone."

A pause longer than the signal lag. "It makes them very good at acquiring resources and very certain they are moving toward something real. The certainty is real. The direction is genuine. He is simply pointed at the wrong thing because no one was there to show him what it actually was."

Quill said, quietly: "So he's the version of you where Nalani doesn't find you."

Jax looked at him. Quill was looking at the table. He had said the thing with the precise flatness of someone who had calculated it to be accurate and delivered it accordingly, without full awareness of how exactly it had landed.

"Yes," Jax said. It was simply true.

Aria-7: "I will integrate this with the resonance data from the intercept. If his interface is active and unguided, and Jax's is increasingly trained and guided, the interaction between them at close range may produce effects beyond what we observed in the debris field engagement. I am modeling this now."

"Continue," Jax said.

## Scene 3 — Two Fronts

He wrote in the journal after the rest of the team had dispersed. The entry was:

*Drayton: Varr bloodline. The version of this where Nalani doesn't find you. The mission just got a different shape.*

He looked at it. Added: *Lena knows something about the accident. About the starting point. She was close to saying what.*

He put the journal away.

He did not connect the two entries. They were in different columns — different problems, different shapes of urgency, different timelines. One strategic. One something else that did not have a category yet, which was a familiar condition. The column of unfiled things had been growing since the rendezvous and it was growing faster, and he had learned on DE that columns like this either resolved on their own schedule or became the problem.

He was fairly certain this one was going to become the problem.

The shard was warm against his sternum. Not engagement-heat — the ordinary warmth of orientation, the quiet signal that meant *moving in the right direction.* He pressed his thumb against the locket's clasp in the way he sometimes did when there was nothing else to do with his hands. The clasp found his thumb and stopped there. He held it. He did not apply pressure.

He sat with the weight of both. Drayton running for twenty years on the fever's drive toward something he could sense and not name. Lena carrying something that was about the accident and about him and almost certainly about the locket in his hand. The Valley still twelve days ahead at current route.

Two things at maximum pressure in two different directions, neither detonating, and the specific quality of the stillness between them — the stillness of things that are very close and have not yet arrived.

At least one of them was going to arrive before he was ready.

He didn't sleep well.
