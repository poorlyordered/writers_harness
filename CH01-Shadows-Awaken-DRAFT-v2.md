# Chapter 1 -- Really Bad Day

## Scene 1 -- The Dream

The flash comes first. Always the flash.

Then the pain: not pain exactly, but the sensation of being erased, every signal cutting at once, every nerve going silent before it can report. Then the nothing.

Not darkness. Jax had learned the difference in the first year, lying on the floor of this same room while the walls did something that felt like spinning. Darkness was something the body knew how to hold. It was an absence of light, which meant it still had edges, still had the particular weight of a thing that existed. The nothing was an absence of everything -- sensation and self and the low hum of being a person -- and it lasted long enough for some part of him to notice that this was not sleep.

He woke.

The ceiling of his quarters was pale concrete, cracked at the same diagonal line it had always been cracked at, carrying the same faint water stain from a leak fixed eight years ago. The air smelled the way it always smelled: metal and mineral dust and the particular flatness of recycled atmosphere that never quite cleared. He lay still. Not to recover -- he was past the stage where recovery was the word for it -- but because he'd learned the floor felt wrong if he moved too fast after the dream.

Three breaths. He counted them the way he had counted them every morning for eleven years: not a ritual so much as a mechanism, a calibration between the nothing and the room. On three he sat up. On four he swung his feet to the floor. The floor felt like the floor.

The tin box was on the shelf where it always was.

Four steps to reach it; he could have made them in the dark. He lifted the lid. Inside: the locket first, because it always appeared to him first -- oval, silver gone warm with handling, the surface worn smooth at the center where his thumb rested without opening it. He had held this locket ten thousand times. He had never opened it. He told himself he was waiting until he knew what was inside, which was a way of saying he was afraid of what was inside, which was a thing he had examined in year two and filed without resolution. The locket was closed. He had work to do.

Beside it, the shard of Stellarium. Dull gray in the pre-shift dark, inert as river stone when it sat in the box, which it always was when it sat in the box. He had stopped wondering about that distinction around year three. The shard was a thing he'd found in his pocket when he woke up on Desolation's Edge with no memory of the eleven years before -- the locket with it, and the note, and nothing else to account for who he was or where he'd been. He'd had the shard tested in his first year. The results had been inconclusive in the specific way that inconclusive meant nothing wrong had been found, which was not the same as nothing wrong being there.

Behind the shard, the note, folded once, in handwriting that matched his own down to the way he closed his e's and left too much space before a period.

You were looking for something. Keep looking.

He'd had the handwriting verified by a specialist in his third year, when he still had the credits and the conviction that certainty would help. An expensive verification that confirmed the note was his and told him nothing about what he'd been looking for or why he'd thought to tell himself about it. The instruction was real. The context was gone. What remained was eleven years of doing what the note said, on a planet where the thing he was looking for was supposed to be found, with a dream that gave him nothing but the nothing and a locket he had never opened.

He closed the box. He had work to do.

The pre-shift meal was habit, not pleasure. He ate at the commissary canteen standing at the counter near the door, whatever was cheapest, because standing near the door meant he could leave without navigating the room. At this hour the canteen held the early-dark set: miners who liked the quiet before the day heated, long-timers who'd been on DE long enough that the shift schedule had rearranged their circadian patterns into something that preferred to start in the cold. He knew their faces. He didn't know most of their names. After enough years they'd stopped trying to change that, and this was the closest thing to comfortable he'd found on Desolation's Edge.

He ate. He left.

Outside, the morning held Xanthius's cold -- dry, without malice, the kind that cracked his knuckles in the first hour of winter and that he'd learned to use as a calibration. If his hands hurt, they were working right. He pulled his collar up and moved toward the mine head at the pace he'd maintained for eleven years: not hurrying, not wasted, the specific efficiency of someone who had learned not to spend what he didn't have to.

Desolation's Edge from the outside had no pretensions. The mine head broke the skyline a kilometer beyond the town's main cluster: crane rigging, ore-processing towers, the two conveyors running parallel toward the crushing facility, everything built for function in a climate that wore everything down. The town existed because the mine existed, the mine existed because Stellarium deposits in the region had drawn enough rumor to keep prospectors coming and enough real ore to keep the corporate contracts renewing. Most people who came to DE looking for Stellarium left with nothing but the years they'd spent looking. Some of them stopped trying to leave.

Jax had stopped trying to leave. He hadn't stopped looking for Stellarium.

## Scene 2 -- The Mines

The mine head at shift change was controlled noise: the outgoing crew moving past the incoming crew still not awake enough to fill the space. Jax checked his equipment at the locker station. Headlamp charge, oxygen reserve, the geological scanner he'd been repairing in pieces for eighteen months because it was the best scanner the Edge had available and the company wouldn't supply a replacement. He'd rebuilt the sensor array twice and the display housing once. Sill, the equipment tech who occupied the corner shop on the main working quarter, had told him on the second rebuild that he was good at fixing things that should have been thrown away. Jax hadn't argued.

The shard he transferred from the tin box to the inside pocket of his jacket, where it traveled close to his ribs during working hours. He'd stopped explaining that habit after year two.

The section assigned to him was a lateral survey of a formation the automated systems had flagged as potentially unstable. Routine work -- the kind that filled the middle hours of a shift and required enough technical attention to keep a person from wandering while leaving the deeper processing available for other things. Jax preferred lateral bores to the deep runs, though the deep runs were where the interesting Stellarium traces had been showing lately. The lateral work gave him what he needed: something the body could do while the rest of him ran the longer calculations in the background. Reading instability in geological formations was something he'd learned the way he'd learned everything on DE -- without instruction, from the rock itself, until the pattern lived in his hands rather than his head. He read stress in stone the way other people read rooms. Continuous, mostly unconscious, registering wrongness before he could name it.

He dropped into the survey section at 0610. The air in a lateral bore was different from the air at depth: not thinner, but closer, the sense of rock pressing in from all sides rather than just above. The walls were pale and fractured in the way of this region's geology, productive and difficult. He moved his scanner at the measured intervals the survey protocol required and made the calls the formation asked for: flagging stress concentrations for the engineering team, annotating the ones that needed escalation, logging the work in the shorthand that eleven years had compressed into something close to its own language.

He was three hours into the second measurement pass when the commotion started forty meters back, toward the junction post.

He heard it before he could parse it: a sharp metallic impact, the specific register of something falling that wasn't supposed to fall, and then a voice that broke wrong underneath it -- not a shout, the more informative sound of someone who had run out of options and hadn't decided yet whether to call for help or keep trying to fix it themselves.

Jax set down his equipment and moved.

The miner was young -- six months on the Edge, maybe seven, still carrying the particular alertness of someone who hadn't yet learned that alertness and preparation were different things. He'd caught his drill brace in a shear clamp that had shifted. Not catastrophic; this was the kind of thing that happened in lateral bores when someone who hadn't learned the formation went faster than it allowed. The drill was running, the brace had locked at the wrong angle, and the miner was torquing against it with the specific desperation of a person who understood the problem clearly enough to know he was making it worse.

If the brace snapped, the drill would kick. At that angle, into that formation, it would kick left. Into the miner's left side at operating speed.

Jax came in from the opposite side of the brace, put his weight against the drill to hold it steady, and found the override lever with his right hand without looking for it. A mechanism most miners knew existed and half had never located -- the gap between knowing a thing and knowing a thing in the dark and under pressure. He found it. He depressed it. The drill disengaged.

The miner straightened, breathing fast. His hands were shaking in the small controlled way hands shook when the body had prepared for something that didn't happen. He opened his mouth.

"Pay attention to the brace angle when the formation's been flagged," Jax said, already turning back toward his equipment. "Shear zones announce themselves. You'll learn to hear them."

He didn't wait for a response. He picked up his scanner and went back to his logging point, noted the interruption in his records without annotation, and ran the last segment of his measurement pass again from the top. The time it had cost him was nine minutes. Not recoverable; he absorbed it and continued.

The rest of the shift ran without incident. He turned in his equipment readings, flagged two annotations as priority, and walked back through the mine head into the afternoon.

## Scene 3 -- End of Day / The Shard

The sun over Desolation's Edge in the late afternoon came in low and amber, the way it always did when Heliosanthus was dropping toward the horizon and the mineral dust in the air caught it. It turned everything the color of old brass: the processing towers, the colony streets, the faces of the end-of-shift workers moving in the same direction at the same pace with the specific shared quality of people who were finished with something and had not yet begun thinking about what came next. The light was particular enough to this world that you couldn't mistake it for anywhere else, which was something Jax had noticed in his first week and kept noticing the way you kept noticing things that were beautiful without comfort.

He walked back through the working quarter at the same pace he'd walked out in the morning. The streets ran parallel to the mine conveyors in the main section, then widened and bent toward the residential blocks on the eastern side. He kept to the working quarter longer than the route required. He had done this for eleven years and did not think of it as avoidance because he had never named it as such.

The Keeper Quarter was at the northeast edge of the residential section, where the street grid lost its regular logic and the buildings became something other than mining infrastructure. Older structures -- or they looked it -- lower and more deliberate in their placement, with plantings that served no practical purpose in this climate and a quality of stillness that persisted through the town's working hours. The people who came and went from them moved differently from the people in the working quarter, unhurried in a way that was not the same as idle. Jax had passed this cluster of buildings on his way to and from the mine for eleven years. He had never stopped. He had noticed, somewhere in year eight or nine, that the people there seemed to notice him back -- not the curiosity of newcomers sizing up a long-timer, something quieter and more deliberate, the way you watched a thing you had been watching for a long time and were still watching.

He passed without stopping, as he always did. He continued to his quarters.

Inside: the same pale concrete ceiling, the same shelf, the same box. He filled a glass of water from the tap and drank it at the window, watching the amber light flatten as Heliosanthus finished its drop. When the light was gone he pulled his tool kit from under the work bench and started on the scanner.

Cleaning equipment after a shift was meditative in the specific way that tasks requiring precision without thought were meditative: the hands busy, attention low, the mind settling into whatever the day had left in it. He had done this for eleven years, tools changing as they wore out and were replaced, the motion always the same. He worked through the scanner's exterior casing, cleared the sensor contacts, ran the diagnostic sequence and noted two readings he'd keep an eye on. The young miner from the lateral bore drifted through once -- the shaking hands, the almost-moment of it. Jax let it drift through and kept working.

The shard hummed.

He stopped.

Barely a sound. Less a sound than a sensation, the way a vibration in the walls of a mine registered in the chest before the ears caught up with it. The shard was in his jacket, draped over the work bench's edge, and the jacket had not moved. He looked at it.

Nothing.

He waited. The room was quiet except for the ambient noise of the colony settling into its evening cycle: somewhere distant a compressor cycling, the low frequency of the recycling units in the walls, a footstep in the corridor that passed and did not return. Nothing that should have set anything off. Nothing that explained what he'd felt.

He crossed to the jacket and took the shard out. Held it flat in his palm the way he always held it, waiting for the response he usually got. Inert. Cool and smooth, the way it always was after a day in his pocket. He turned it over once. Pressed his thumb along its longest edge. Nothing.

He stood there for a moment longer than he needed to.

Then he set the shard on the shelf beside the tin box, went back to the bench, and finished cleaning his tools.

He didn't open the tin box again that night. He had work to do in the morning, and the dream would come back whether he spent the evening thinking about it or whether he slept.

He slept.
