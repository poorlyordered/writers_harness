# Chapter 5 -- Stasis = Death

## Scene 1 -- Back in the Mines

The shift had its rhythm and he stepped back into it the way you stepped back into a current: not choosing to swim, just standing where the water was.

The northeastern shafts were his assignment for the week -- the follow-up survey he'd flagged before the off-shift, the two scanner readings he'd been watching, the annotation backlog he'd told himself he'd clear before the weekend and hadn't. He cleared it in the first hour. The scanner readings resolved by the third. By midday he was running a standard survey corridor two sections over from the anomaly shaft, logging formations with the shorthand he'd developed in year three that the company's systems would flag and a later reader would correctly interpret, which was all documentation needed to do.

He worked alongside Ferris for the better part of the afternoon.

Ferris had been at Desolation's Edge for thirty years -- Jax knew this the way long-timers knew each other's tenure, filed with the other facts about a person that accumulated without asking and held without necessarily acting on. He was in his sixties, built like someone who had done physical work for all of those thirty years and expected to do physical work for whatever time remained without particularly minding, which was an accurate description as far as Jax could tell. He moved through the shaft with the efficiency of a man who had stopped wasting effort at some point in his past and had been practicing that economy ever since. He did not ask unnecessary questions. He did not offer unrequested information. Jax had always found him easy to work next to.

They were running parallel cores in the middle section -- the kind of work that required attention and didn't require conversation -- when Ferris said: "What brought you out here, originally?"

Jax didn't look up from the readout. "Work."

"Sure." A pause while Ferris extracted a core segment and bagged it with the ease of long practice. "But what specifically."

"Stellarium." The standard answer. Not untrue. "Heard the region was producing. Came to look."

Ferris nodded the nod of a man who had received this answer in various forms many times and found it consistent with his understanding of how things went. "And then you stayed."

"Work was good."

"That's how it goes," Ferris said. Not with irony. Not with resignation either -- the simple tone of someone stating an obvious thing, a fact of the world as basic as the geology under their boots. That's how it goes. He went back to the core sample. Jax went back to the readout. The shift continued.

Jax did not look at the man next to him after that. He noted, briefly and without elaboration, that Ferris was good at his work and had been good at his work for thirty years and would probably be good at his work for whatever years his body held out, and that this was a complete life in the way that any life was complete -- full from one end to the other with the texture of itself. Good at the craft. Decent to work with. Stories that had a radius: the mines, the colony, some years spent elsewhere before he'd stopped going elsewhere. A man who had come here for a reason and stayed because staying had become the answer.

He noted it. He set it down. He picked up his tool and went back to the survey.

The formations in this section were unremarkable: standard banded silicates, the kind of geology that produced steady ore yields without surprises. He logged them accurately and moved through the corridor at the pace the survey required. By the time the shift whistle sounded, he had fourteen clean entries in the log, the anomalies resolved and annotated, a core sample set bagged and labeled for the crusher facility. Good work. A full day of it.

He walked back through the shaft in the amber light that collected there at shift's end, the dust thick with particulates, the mine carrying its familiar smell -- stone and machine oil and the faint metallic edge that he'd stopped noticing years ago except when he tried to remember when he'd stopped noticing it. Ferris was a few meters ahead of him, unhurried, carrying his tools with the exact economy of a man who had long since calculated the precise amount of effort the day required and was not in the habit of exceeding it.

*That's how it goes.*

Jax kept walking. The shaft opened into the main corridor, the light changed, the noise of the processing towers came back. He filed the observation in the same category as everything else he filed when he could not use it, and the filing was complete, and he walked on.

## Scene 2 -- The Keeper Quarter Conversation

The route he walked off-shift had not changed.

He was aware of this in the specific way you were aware of something you'd been doing automatically and had recently begun to notice again. The colony route that took him past the Keeper Quarter. He could adjust it. He hadn't. This was a fact he held without examining.

The Keeper Quarter at this hour was in the last of the afternoon light -- the amber that belonged to Desolation's Edge and no other place, the light that the mine dust made out of whatever Heliosanthus was made of, which he had always found specific and impossible to explain to anyone who hadn't seen it. The buildings in the Quarter caught it differently than the residential blocks. Held it, somehow. As if the air in that cluster of older structures had a different relationship with moving on. He'd noticed this before in the undirected way you noticed things you passed regularly without deciding to look at.

A resident was outside. Not the woman with the sealant tool, and not the older man who had watched him turn and walk away -- someone different, younger, late thirties, moving with the particular economy of motion that some people developed in proximity to something large: a slight conservation of gesture, an attention that processed more than it acknowledged. He was trimming back a plant that grew in a container near the entrance to one of the buildings. The kind of work that was mostly done, but that gave you something to do with your hands.

He looked up when Jax was level with him. He didn't call out.

Jax didn't stop walking. His pace didn't accelerate either.

"Long shift?" the resident said. Conversational. The way you asked a neighbor, not the way you asked someone you'd been watching.

"Standard." Jax's pace held.

"Northeast shafts," the resident said. Not a question.

This was true. It was also a thing you could know by watching people walk toward the mine head in the morning and noting which direction they turned. Jax filed it and kept moving. "Good survey territory."

"Mm." The resident went back to the plant for a moment, pinching off a dead section at the stem with the two-fingered precision of someone who had done this before. Then, in the same easy register: "My grandmother used to say that about Stellarium. That it finds the territory it wants. That the people it's already decided on don't choose their route -- they just think they do."

Jax's attention spiked.

He kept walking.

The spike was brief and complete -- the kind of internal response that arrived before interpretation could catch up, something in the words landing at a different weight than their surface suggested, his body registering it before his mind identified what. He identified what at five or six steps' distance. The phrasing. *Already decided. Don't choose.* The structural position of that thought -- identical to what Nalani had said, in different words, different register, the same argument placed the same way. *You have always been looking. That is not going to stop.*

He found the safest interpretation and held it.

The Keeper Quarter families had been on Desolation's Edge longer than anyone else in the population. Frontier communities developed Stellarium folklore -- that was documented, consistent across planetary territories, a known feature of resource-extraction settlements that operated near active mineral zones. The projection through the shard had real mechanics; those mechanics did not operate in a vacuum. The language Nalani had used would draw on whatever tradition existed in the place she was reaching. The resident's grandmother's saying was the tradition. Nalani's words were the tradition's application.

This was the explanation. It was complete. It accounted for everything.

He filed it.

Behind him, without looking back, he was aware that the resident had stopped trimming and was watching him walk. The awareness was not accusation -- nothing in the evening's exchange had been accusation -- but he was conscious of it as a thing, the way you were conscious of weather at your back. He kept moving. He had the explanation. He had the full shift's work behind him and the morning shift ahead of him and a set of annotations to review before he slept. The Keeper Quarter was behind him and then further behind him and then at the distance where you stopped tracking things that were behind you.

He walked that distance and kept going for a few steps more, because the not-tracking was its own kind of tracking, and he was aware of that, and he wanted to be past both of them before he stopped.

## Scene 3 -- Quarters / The Tin Box

He ate at Okafor's -- the corner booth with the short leg that rocked if you put your elbows on it, which he'd established early that he did not do. He ate without talking to anyone, which was the usual arrangement. Three tables over, a group from the mid-shift coring crew was working through some yield discrepancy loud enough to be audible. He tracked this in the way he tracked all canteen noise: present, filed, not retained.

He went back to his quarters.

The tin box was on the shelf.

He was aware of it the way you were aware of something you had decided, provisionally and without announcement, not to look at this evening. He set his jacket on the back of the chair and washed his hands at the basin and looked at the survey notes he'd transferred to his personal record during the late break, confirmed they matched the formal log, and put them with the others in the folder on the desk.

Then he got out the journal.

The journal had been his record system since year one -- not the mine logs, which were company property and filed accordingly, but the personal record that lived in the desk drawer and held the things the official logs didn't. Fragment observations. Shard behavior notes, brief and increasingly infrequent, categorized under *unexplained -- not actively investigated*. The dream, at irregular intervals when the standard entry felt insufficient. Events he'd been present for that the formal record didn't require.

He opened to the current page and wrote:

*The dream again. Not adding up to anything.*

He sat with the pen for a moment. He was aware that this was not a complete account of the last several days. He was aware of what the complete account would include: the sustained pulse, the warmth, the woman in his quarters in the afternoon light who had spoken his name and named everything in the tin box without being told and delivered a prophecy he had correctly refused. The Keeper Quarter, twice, third encounter tonight, the resident's grandmother's saying that had spiked his attention and then been correctly explained and filed. The older miner, thirty years, *that's how it goes*.

He was aware of what a complete account would look like and of the specific decision not to write it. Not as omission exactly. As the practical conclusion that a complete account would not be a useful record. He had no category for any of it. He could not write usefully about things he had no category for. The point of the journal was useful records.

*Northeast survey complete. Anomalies resolved. Clean shift.*

He closed the journal and put it in the drawer.

The tin box was still on the shelf. He could see it without looking at it, the peripheral awareness of a thing at the edge of attention. The shard was inside it. The locket was inside it, sealed, his thumb having found the latch the other day and set it back down, not because anyone had told him to wait but because he wasn't ready and not being ready was a thing he was allowed to be. The note was inside it: *you were looking for something, keep looking*, in his own handwriting, verified by a specialist in year three, his own words from a time he could not reach.

He had work in the morning. The northeast shafts, the follow-up cores, the second round of scanner coverage he'd started today and would complete tomorrow. He knew the section. He knew the work. He would be there at first shift and he would do the work correctly, because he had done the work correctly for eleven years and expected to continue.

He didn't open the tin.

He turned off the desk light and went to his bunk and lay down in the specific way that accommodated the bunk's inadequacies, and he looked at the ceiling in the dark, and after a while the amber outside his window went to nothing, and the mine head went quiet for the shift change, and the quiet had its own sound, and eventually that resolved too.

He had work in the morning. That was the relevant fact. Everything else -- the tin box with its contents, the Keeper Quarter and its patient residents, the woman who had stood in his quarters in the afternoon light and said *you have always been looking, and that is not going to stop* -- everything else was the residue of recent days. It would settle. Things settled. He was very good at giving them the conditions to settle.

He went to sleep.
