## Scene 1 -- Before the Attack

The landing pads at Desolation's Edge ran along the colony's eastern edge, three platforms of reinforced composite that had been gradually settling into the ground for eleven years. Platform two had a seam near its western edge where the substrate had shifted; Jax had learned the step-over the same week he arrived and had never thought about it since. He did not think about it now. He stepped over and walked toward the ship at the near strut without checking his footing.

Tobias Blackwood was leaning against the landing strut with a card between two fingers, not quite running it back and forth, not quite still. He had the easy posture of someone who had mastered the specific art of looking like he was not waiting for anything. He was wearing a flight jacket that had been good quality once and was now good quality in the way that things are when they have been repaired enough times to constitute a different object. Green eyes, tousled brown hair, the faint smile of a man who had decided some time ago that the world was more interesting if he treated it as slightly amusing.

"You could take the long loop," Toby said, as if they were already in the middle of a conversation. "Solara, then Kepler's Transit, pick up the Meridian cargo at the secondary hub. Three weeks out. Good margins, nobody asking uncomfortable questions about routing windows."

"I'm not going anywhere," Jax said.

Toby turned the card over without looking at it. "Right. You mentioned."

"I'm on the eastern face this cycle. Reiss has me on secondary for the next three shifts."

"Did also mention that." The card disappeared between his fingers and reappeared on the other side. He had the hands of someone who had once learned card tricks to have something to do and had continued long past the point where it was necessary. "I'm just saying the route exists. As a data point. For a man who is not going anywhere and is fully committed to that position."

Jax looked at him.

"Data point," Toby said again. Mild. "No agenda."

He had known Toby Blackwood for going on four years -- not well, in the way that people at a regular waypoint are never quite well-known, but in the way that the specific shape of a person becomes familiar. Toby moved cargo on irregular routes through marginal systems and had been running through DE long enough that his presence at the pads was unremarkable. He was the kind of person you trusted because he had never given you a reason not to, which was different from the kind of person you trusted because you understood them. Jax had understood for a long time that these were different things.

The easy manner covered something Jax had never been able to fully read. Accurate observation, certainly -- Toby noticed things and chose not to demonstrate that he had noticed them. Whether that was courtesy or leverage held in reserve, Jax had never been certain. The ambiguity was consistent enough that it had become part of who Toby was, at least in the version of Toby that Jax had access to.

Across the pad, near the secondary struts, a woman was doing a walkthrough of the equipment staging area.

Short spiky red hair. Brown eyes. The deliberate economy of movement that came from years of reading physical environments for threat angles -- not the practiced awareness of someone who had learned it from a manual but the worn-in awareness of someone who had needed it often enough that it had stopped being a skill and become a posture. She moved through the staging area the way Jax moved through the mine's secondary corridors: without appearing to check anything, while checking everything.

He had seen her at intervals over the past week. Security contract with one of the subcontractors working the colony's southwest section -- not DE's own operation but adjacent to it, the kind of arrangement that was common on extraction sites where the infrastructure was shared and the liability wasn't. Her contract ended today. She was doing the end-of-contract walkthrough: equipment return, final perimeter confirmation, the administrative close that converted an active engagement into a completed one.

"Stavros," Toby said, without inflection, as if identifying something Jax had been looking at.

"I know."

"Good at what she does." He tilted his head slightly in her direction, not enough to be looking at her. "Very efficient ending to a contract."

The shard was doing what it had done every day since his return from Sanctuary Valley: a steady directional pulse southwest, patient and consistent, pointing toward the choice he had not made. He had adjusted to it. It had taken active effort in the first two days, less effort in the days following, and had settled into the background the way the platform seam had settled into the background -- catalogued, present, the kind of constant that a person learns to step around rather than examine.

He registered the pulse. He did not act on it. He looked at the ship and thought about the eastern face and the shift rotation and the three weeks of Toby's route that he was not taking.

Then the pulse changed.

Not louder. Not the escalating warmth from the shrine, not the directional strengthening. Something shifted in the quality of it, a texture he had not felt before -- the difference between a sound at steady volume and the same sound at a different frequency. He did not have a name for it. What he had was the awareness that something had changed, and the instinct, immediate and certain, that the change was relevant.

Toby looked up at the sky to the southwest. His hands had stopped moving. The card was still.

Mira Stavros stopped walking. She did not turn toward anything yet -- her weight just redistributed, the subtle shift of someone whose body was receiving information slightly ahead of what the mind had caught up to.

The first impact came three seconds later.


## Scene 2 -- The Attack

It was not a raid. He understood that within the first fifteen seconds.

A raid was diffuse -- volume, disruption, take what you can and move before the cost got too high. What came over the colony's perimeter was targeted. He watched the team split before they cleared the wall: one broke hard toward the processing facility, one covered the residential lane at an angle that suggested prior knowledge of where the lane opened and where it narrowed, and one came directly toward Jax's position with the efficiency of a man who had been given a specific task and understood what completion looked like.

Jax had eleven years of miner's instinct about structural leverage and how bodies moved through confined space. The man was good -- well-trained, fast, reading the angles as he approached. He was also committed to a line, and Jax was already inside it before the second step landed. What followed was brief and close and expensive for the man, who went down hard and stayed down. Jax had already turned to read the next problem.

Two more came over the eastern wall.

The shard flared.

Not the pulse. Not the warmth he had learned to catalogue. This was different in kind from anything he had felt from it before: a heat that was not temperature, a pressure that moved from his sternum outward through his chest and down his right arm and through his right hand and then it was over. His arm had moved. He had been aware, distantly and without sufficient information to make the awareness useful, that something was happening. It happened. It was over.

Both men who had come over the wall were no longer standing where they had been standing.

They hadn't fallen. They had gone back -- a foot, two feet, stumbling in the way of something struck by a force applied from the direction where Jax was standing. They were down. Jax's right hand was slightly raised, which he had not directed it to do, and the air between him and the wall was the same air it had been.

He stood for two seconds.

There were still threats in his peripheral awareness. He filed the two seconds under *later* and moved.

The processing facility side was loud -- the team member who had broken that direction had run into workers who had been mid-shift and had not retreated in the direction expected. Jax heard the collision before he reached the facility wall and came in through the secondary entrance at an angle that let him read the room before the room read him. Three workers, two ground personnel, the close-quarters arithmetic of a space where knowing the wall geometry mattered more than anything else. He knew the wall geometry. He had been in this room in various states of repair for eleven years.

Toby's ship moved.

It was not a heroic gesture. He would not have called it that and it did not look like one. A pilot had read what the ship could contribute to a situation and had contributed it: the ship repositioned between the residential cluster and the secondary approach vector, and the shadow it threw across the landing pad changed the geometry of what the remaining ground forces could see and what angles they could take without walking into exposure they hadn't planned for. No announcement. No acknowledgment from anyone. The ship moved and the situation was different and that was the whole of it.

Mira Stavros had handled the second-team contact before Jax cleared the facility. He came out of the secondary entrance and saw the aftermath: one man down, a second retreating toward the eastern perimeter in the way of someone who had encountered significantly more resistance than the intelligence had accounted for. She was already moving to the next position, already reading the angle from the residential lane, and the fact that her contract had ended an hour ago had introduced no visible hesitation into any decision she had made since the first impact. Her training was not a thing she turned on. It was a thing she had become.

The ground team began to withdraw.

Organized. On schedule. Not because they had been driven off in disarray but because the withdrawal had been built into the operation -- they had accomplished the objective, or determined that the cost of continuing exceeded the parameters they had been given. They had come here for something specific. Jax had been in the way of that something, not the target of it. The withdrawal confirmed the objective had been reached or abandoned on terms that had nothing to do with him.

He registered that. He filed it next to the two seconds and the thing that had happened with his right hand.

The attack ended.

The colony made the specific silence of a place that had just been through something.

Something had happened. He did not examine what.


## Scene 3 -- After

He walked the perimeter twice.

The first pass was operational: damage assessment, position confirmation, the systematic accounting of what the attack had produced. The processing facility had lost a section of its eastern wall -- the interior team had reached the emergency shutdown before any critical systems were compromised, and the workers who had been inside were out and accounted for and functional in the ways that mattered. Two injuries, neither requiring more than the colony's own medical resources. No deaths. That was not the guaranteed outcome given what had been present on the pad, and he registered it with the specific weight that no-deaths always carried when it was the exception rather than the expectation.

The second pass was not operational. He did not name what it was.

DE was quiet in the way a place is quiet after violence, which is a different quiet from ordinary evening. The ordinary evening quiet at DE was the quiet of a place doing what it had always done -- the distant machinery noise, the shift-change traffic between the residential blocks and the staging areas, the sulfur note from the eastern smelter that had been a background constant for eleven years. All of that was still present. The smelter had not paused for the attack; the machinery was running or cycling down in its ordinary patterns; the particulate haze caught the early-evening light exactly as it always did.

What was different was underneath the familiar sounds. A place that had felt stable because it had always been stable had been replaced with a place that was familiar because the geography had not moved. Those were not the same stability. The first kind had a weight to it, a depth, the quality of something that had been true for a long time and intended to remain true. The second was the surface of a thing that the thing no longer held.

He completed the second pass and stood at the eastern wall section near where the first contact had been. The composite had a section of stress fracture from the second-phase pressure; he ran his hand along it and felt what the material had absorbed.

The shard was warm in his pocket. Baseline -- not the flare, not the alert-quality, not the southwest directional pull that had been his background constant for nine days. Just the ordinary warmth. He was aware, in a way he did not engage with directly, that the flare had not left a residue. Whatever had happened in the two seconds with the two men and his right hand had happened and was finished and the shard had returned to baseline as if returning from an ordinary use.

He was aware this was not reassuring.

Toby found him there. He came around the corner of the staging area from the direction of the pads, card in hand, unhurried. He did not approach with the energy of someone who had been through something. He approached with the energy of someone who had been through something and had already decided where he stood.

"Ship's fine," he said. "Took one near-miss on the hull panel, port side. Already assessed. Cosmetic." He leaned against the wall section at an angle that was not quite casual but was performing casual as a courtesy to the situation. "I'm between loads right now. Flexible condition."

Jax looked at the stress fracture in the composite.

"Was planning to route through Kepler's Transit," Toby said. "Timeline on that is what you'd call adjustable."

Jax said nothing.

"I'm just observing that the ship is here and I'm not immediately obligated to be anywhere else." He turned the card over. "Circumstances being what they are."

It was not a heroic offer. It was the same practical read he had brought to repositioning the ship -- here is what I have, here is what the situation might need, here is whether those things align. Toby Blackwood had never given Jax a reason not to trust him. He had also never made himself fully legible. Right now, standing at the eastern wall with a card in his hand and his timeline adjusted, what he was offering was a ship and a presence and the specific kind of reliability that came from a man who had shown up when it cost something to show up.

Jax was aware that he did not fully understand Toby Blackwood. He was aware that not fully understanding a person and needing what they were offering were not mutually exclusive conditions.

Mira Stavros appeared from the direction of the staging area. She was carrying what was left of her gear -- the section of the pad near the secondary struts had absorbed some of the facility wall in the second phase, and a portion of the equipment she had been logging out had been in that section. The contract was already administratively void. The equipment records were somewhere in the wreckage. None of this appeared to be the thing she was currently thinking about.

She looked at Jax. The direct gaze of someone who had finished running an assessment and arrived at a conclusion and was waiting for the conversation to catch up to what she had already decided.

She did not state the conclusion. She stayed.

He had spent nine days trying to preserve the ordinary world. He had done it through daily, active, effortful maintenance -- the journals he didn't update, the shard pulse he adjusted around, the questions in the Keeper Quarter he didn't ask, the route through Kepler's Transit he didn't take. He had held the position. He had held it past the point where he believed the position was correct, because the position had been the only structure available and he had been unwilling to give up the structure until there was something else to stand on.

The ordinary world had not required his permission to stop being ordinary.

The choice was no longer whether to leave. He was already standing in the place that leaving would have been leaving from, and the place had changed, and the argument for staying had been destroyed along with the section of wall.

"I need a ship," he said.

Toby turned the card over. "Funny you should mention that."

Mira was looking at him with the expression of someone for whom the answer had been clear since approximately the first impact. Not impatience -- she was not waiting for him to catch up. She was simply already where she was going and prepared to proceed from there.

The shard was warm at baseline in his pocket, pointing southwest.

He had work to do.

For the first time in nine days, that was not the problem.
