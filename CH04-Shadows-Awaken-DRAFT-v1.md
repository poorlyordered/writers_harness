# Chapter 4 -- Grasping at Straws

## Scene 1 -- The Projection Arrives

The afternoon off-shift had the quality they all had: too quiet for the mind to settle and too long to let it wander without consequence. Jax was at his desk with his journal open, writing about the woman in the mine in the methodical way he wrote about things he had not yet resolved -- building the account from the factual end, letting the facts accumulate until a category emerged to hold them. He had been writing for twenty minutes. No category had emerged.

The shard pulsed.

He stopped writing.

Not the brief pulse from the mine. Not even the three-beat sequence that had sent him northeast through someone else's claim. This was sustained -- a rhythm that continued, regular as a heartbeat, steady enough that he could not pretend he had imagined the first one and wait for it to stop. He set down his pen. He crossed to the shelf and opened the tin box and picked up the shard without the waiting pause, without the ceremony of holding it flat on his palm to see if today was the day it would give him nothing. He already knew today was not that day.

The shard was warm.

He had been carrying this piece of mineral for eleven years. It had never been warm. He held it for a moment and let the fact of it settle. Warm, and pulsing, and in the afternoon light coming through the narrow window his quarters had been allocated, the gray of it was no longer quite the gray of river stone. There was something under the surface. Not a glow -- something more interior than that, the way certain rocks held the day's heat after dark: warmth that had been gathered rather than produced.

She was there.

Not in the room in any material sense that would have satisfied a measurement -- no displacement of air, no shadow cast, no surface her weight would have required. Present there, between the desk and the window, in a way that preceded any explanation he might have tried to build. He had seen her in the mine at the far edge of lantern light, translucent and uncertain. This was different. In his quarters, in afternoon light, with the shard warm in his closed hand, she was clearer than she had been. Not solid -- but defined. Small. White hair loose. The warm brown of her eyes specific enough that he could not have invented the color.

She looked at him with the patience of someone who had been waiting for this conversation for a long time and was not going to rush it now that it had arrived. Not urgency. Not relief. Simply the settled quality of a person who had known a thing was going to happen and had been correct about it, which was a different kind of satisfaction from surprise.

She said his name.

Just that. Jax. The way you said a name when you were opening something rather than calling for something. And then she began.

## Scene 2 -- She Knows

"The tin box is on the shelf behind you," she said. Her voice had the quality of her presence -- unhurried, without performance, landing the way certain facts landed rather than the way certain arguments landed. "Three items. The locket, which you have never opened. The shard, which you are holding. And a note."

He did not confirm or deny this.

"The note is in your handwriting," she said. "It says: You were looking for something. Keep looking. You have had it verified. It is yours. You do not know when you wrote it."

He had not told anyone about the verification. He had not told anyone about the note's contents. He stood in his quarters in the afternoon light with the warm shard in his hand and let this fact complete itself.

She spoke a word then. One word, two syllables, in a language he did not recognize -- not one he had failed to place but one that was simply not in any category of language he had encountered in eleven years across DE's population. The word was for the shard. He understood that without being told -- the word and the object it belonged to arrived together in his comprehension, the way a true name arrived rather than a label. The shard in his hand had been called something before it was called nothing, before it was called a mineral fragment of unknown composition. The word she had spoken was what it had always been called, in a language older than this sector's recorded history.

He looked at her.

"The locket," she said, and her voice changed register -- not softer, but more careful, the way you handled something that could bear weight but needed to be placed correctly. "What it holds belongs to a life you cannot reach from here. The locket knows what it is and where it belongs. When the time comes, you will open it. The meaning will be available to you then. I will not describe the meaning now, because the description would not help and would possibly harm."

She paused. He had not spoken. She did not appear to need him to.

"Sanctuary Valley is real," she said. "It exists on this planet, within travel distance of where you are standing. I have been waiting for you specifically -- not someone like you, not a person matching your description. You. The prophecy I have spent my life in service to is not a symbol or an archetype. It is about a man, and the man is you, and what is coming requires that I explain this to you in person because what it requires cannot be transmitted through the shard." She paused again, the way someone paused when they were not uncertain but were choosing the weight of what came next. "You are needed."

Jax said, "No."

He meant it. Not I'll consider it and not convince me further -- no, plain and complete, the same word he used when he had already built the answer before the question finished arriving. He was a miner. He had a shift tomorrow and survey logs to annotate tonight and a scanner with two readings he needed to watch. He did not follow apparitions to hidden valleys. He did not accept missions from presences he could not verify. The fact that she knew the contents of his tin box was not evidence of trustworthiness -- it was evidence of something, and he did not yet know what, and until he knew what it was, it sat in the same category as everything else he filed when he could not explain it: noted, not pursued.

"I understand," she said.

She was not discouraged. He could see that plainly -- not the bravado of someone maintaining a position, but genuine untroubled acceptance of his answer. She had expected this. She found it reasonable. Her timeline was longer than this conversation, and she was operating from it.

"You have already been looking," she said, and her voice had something in it then that was not quite warmth and not quite recognition but sat between them. "You have always been looking. That is not going to stop."

She was not arguing with his refusal. She was placing a true thing next to it, the way you placed a stone next to a line to mark where it was -- not to move the line, but to remember. Then she was gone. The shard in his hand went cool, and then still, and the afternoon light fell through the window the same way it always fell, and his quarters were his quarters again.

He stood there for a moment with the shard in his hand and the warmth draining out of it.

## Scene 3 -- Toward the Keeper Quarter

He sat down at his desk.

The journal was open to the page he'd been writing on. The account of the mine apparition: two days ago, northeastern shaft, beyond the boundary marker. He picked up his pen and looked at it and set it back down. He was not going to write about this yet. He did not have the category for it and writing before the category arrived produced records that were less useful than the absence of records.

He opened the tin box instead.

The locket. Oval, silver, the surface worn where his thumb always rested. She had said he would open it when the time came. He turned it in his fingers. He thought about opening it now -- not because it was the right time but because someone had told him there was a right time, and he had a standing objection to being managed, even by true things. His thumb found the latch.

He set it back.

Not yet. Not because she had told him not to, but because he wasn't ready, and being not ready was different from being told to wait. He was allowed to not be ready. He put the locket back, looked at the note, read it the way he had read it ten thousand times without the reading changing anything.

You were looking for something. Keep looking.

He closed the tin. He sat for a few more minutes. Then he went outside, because sitting had run its course and outside was what came next when sitting ran its course.

The colony in the late afternoon had the amber quality it always had at this hour, everything caught in the mineral light, the mine head structures and the processing towers and the residential blocks all rendered in the same specific gold that belonged to this world and no other. He walked without directing his feet, which was how he walked when he had more to process than his attention could hold. His feet knew DE the way they knew the survey corridors: without consultation.

He stopped at the edge of the Keeper Quarter.

He had not decided to come here. He was here. The cluster of older buildings, the plantings, the quality of the light that collected between the structures rather than passing through. A different resident than the woman with the sealant tool -- someone older, seated on the low step outside the nearest building, doing nothing in particular. Not waiting in the anxious sense. Sitting the way a rock sat in a current: present without effort.

The resident looked up at him. Said nothing.

Jax said nothing.

The Keeper Quarter was ten meters in front of him. Whatever it held was ten meters in front of him. The woman in the mine and the woman doing repair work on this door frame had looked at him the same way, and an apparition in his quarters had known the contents of his tin box and named the shard in a language that predated his sector's history. Ten meters.

He stood there for the length of time it took to decide and not decide and decide again.

Then he turned and walked back to his quarters.

He had work to do in the morning. The survey logs needed annotation and the scanner had two readings he was watching and the annotation for today's shift was still in his head rather than on the record where it belonged. He had work to do.

He did not know if the looking the note meant was the looking he had been doing. He had always assumed they were the same thing. He was less certain of that now.
